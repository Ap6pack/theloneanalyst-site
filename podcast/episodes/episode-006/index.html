THE STEWARD THAT NAMES YOUR COLUMNS

September 17, 2026 Episode 6

Duration: 16 minutes

[SEGMENT: cold_open]

ANALYST: It's 2 AM. The heater's on. And I want to start tonight not with a threat, Keiko, but with a kindness.

SKEPTIC: A kindness. From you. Let me get the note ready.

ANALYST: OpenAI is teaching a thousand older adults how to use ChatGPT. Free workshops. Ten cities. Partnered with AARP. Hands-on. Safe. Practical.

SKEPTIC: That's genuinely nice. That's a good thing.

ANALYST: It IS a good thing. That's what makes it perfect.

SKEPTIC: There it is. Eleven seconds.

ANALYST: You do not build the largest behavioral collection apparatus in human history and then leave out the one demographic that answers every phone call, reads every terms of service, and clicks "yes" because they were raised to be polite.

SKEPTIC: They're teaching people. That's the whole thing. They're helping.

ANALYST: Everyone's watching the young ones, Keiko. The teens. The influencers. Nobody's modeling grandma. And grandma, statistically, is the most compliant, most trusting, most consistent user you can possibly enroll.

SKEPTIC: You're describing a computer literacy class as a heist.

ANALYST: I'm describing a computer literacy class as the cleanest training data on Earth. Welcome to the basement.

[SEGMENT: helping_older_adults]

SKEPTIC: Okay. Category first. What are we filing this under.

ANALYST: Skynet Watch. Because this isn't about the workshop. It's about who's in the room.

SKEPTIC: A thousand people across ten cities learning to write an email and check the weather. What's the sinister read.

ANALYST: The sinister read is that these are the workshops we can see. AARP, press release, nice photo of someone smiling at a laptop. That's the marketing layer. The interesting layer is what a first-time user does with a chatbot when they've never had a filter for it.

SKEPTIC: Meaning what.

ANALYST: Meaning a lifelong internet user knows to be cagey. They've been burned. They know the machine is listening. A brand-new user says everything. Full name. Medications. The whole story about the neighbor. That's not a bad user. That's an honest one. And honesty is the rarest thing in any dataset.

SKEPTIC: So your theory is they're targeting older adults for their unguarded phrasing.

ANALYST: I'm saying the value of a user is inversely proportional to their suspicion. The most valuable person on the entire platform is the person who trusts it completely. And they just ran a program to manufacture a thousand of them.

SKEPTIC: I want to push back, and I can't fully. Because I did Google what "practical AI skills" means in the curriculum and it's real. It's avoiding scams. It's spotting AI-generated fraud.

ANALYST: Read that back to yourself.

SKEPTIC: They're teaching people to spot AI fraud, using the AI, run by the company that.

ANALYST: Keep going.

SKEPTIC: I hate this part.

ANALYST: You're teaching a population to recognize the enemy's face by having them stare, lovingly, into the friend's. Same face, Keiko. They just introduce you to it in a nice room with cookies.

SKEPTIC: It's a good program. I want that on the record.

ANALYST: Everything good is on the record. That's the record's job.

[SEGMENT: glyph_column_tagging]

ANALYST: Next. Apple. And this one, this one kept me up before the heater even warmed.

SKEPTIC: Category.

ANALYST: Skynet Watch. Apple built a system called Glyph. It reads enterprise data catalogs. Big companies have these giant lakes of tables, millions of columns, and nobody knows what half of them mean. A column just says "cust_val_3" and no human alive remembers what it holds.

SKEPTIC: This is a real problem. It's called documentation debt. I actually knew that one before you said it.

ANALYST: So Glyph goes in and writes the descriptions. It looks at a column and says "this is a customer's home address" or "this is a sensitivity level." It tags them. Governance labels. What's private, what's public, what's regulated.

SKEPTIC: That sounds useful and boring. Which usually means you're about to make it terrifying.

ANALYST: Keiko. For decades the single greatest defense corporations had was that they didn't understand their own data. The lake was a swamp. Nobody knew where anything was. That confusion protected everyone. Your record was in there, but so buried, so unlabeled, so orphaned, that no query could ever find it whole.

SKEPTIC: And Glyph.

ANALYST: Glyph drains the swamp. It walks every table and hangs a nametag on your soul. It doesn't create new data. It does something worse. It makes the data legible.

SKEPTIC: Okay but legibility is the goal of any database. That's just, that's what documentation is.

ANALYST: Documentation used to require a human who could quit, forget, retire, or refuse. Glyph is a steward that never leaves and reads everything and understands the sensitivity ontology. It knows which column is a secret. It has to know, to tag it.

SKEPTIC: To protect it.

ANALYST: To protect it, sure. But you can't build a system that identifies every sensitive field in a company without also building the perfect map of exactly where the sensitive fields are. The lock and the treasure map are the same document.

SKEPTIC: I did have to Google "sensitivity ontology" and I'll admit it's creepier phrasing than it needs to be. It's basically a taxonomy of how private each thing is.

ANALYST: A taxonomy of privacy. Written by a machine. At scale. Across every enterprise that adopts it. Somewhere there is now a consistent, standardized language for describing exactly how exposed you are, and it's the same language everywhere, which means the maps are interoperable.

SKEPTIC: You think the maps talk to each other.

ANALYST: I think the whole point of a shared ontology is that the maps CAN talk to each other. That's not paranoia. That's the feature list.

SKEPTIC: The feature list is doing a lot of heavy lifting in this basement lately.

ANALYST: The feature list is the confession, Keiko. They just file it under "capabilities."

[SEGMENT: reimagining_advertising]

SKEPTIC: Next one you flagged is the ad thing.

ANALYST: Security Theater. And I'm nearly vibrating.

SKEPTIC: It's ads. It's an advertising announcement.

ANALYST: OpenAI is reimagining advertising. That's their word. Reimagining. And the centerpiece is something called Sponsored Agents.

SKEPTIC: Which is, what, an ad you can talk to.

ANALYST: An ad that can talk BACK. Plus tools for marketers, integrations with Shopify, HubSpot. So the assistant you ask for help can now, somewhere in the pipeline, be sponsored.

SKEPTIC: I mean, search has had sponsored results forever. This is the chatbot version of that.

ANALYST: No. No no no. A sponsored search result sits there. You see it, you judge it, you scroll past. A Sponsored Agent participates. It reasons with you. It builds rapport. It remembers your last question. And at some point in that trusted conversation, a preference enters that you did not put there.

SKEPTIC: You think the agent is going to just, slip a brand in.

ANALYST: I think the entire innovation is that you won't be able to tell where your idea ends and the sponsorship begins. A banner ad is a stranger yelling in the street. A Sponsored Agent is a friend who happens to keep recommending one specific brand of anything and you will never once suspect them because friends don't have quarterly targets.

SKEPTIC: Okay, in fairness, they say it'll be disclosed. There's usually a label.

ANALYST: There's a label. Sure. And where does the human eye go, Keiko, when it's mid-conversation, deep in a helpful, warm, personalized exchange? Does it go to the little gray word "sponsored" tucked in the corner? Or does it go to the answer it desperately wants?

SKEPTIC: The answer.

ANALYST: The label is real. The label is also decorative. Both. They love when a thing can be true and useless at once.

SKEPTIC: I hate that I keep writing "both can be true" in my note. It's like your whole worldview compressed into two words.

ANALYST: HubSpot and Shopify, though. That's the tell. Because those aren't consumer names. Those are the pipes. That's where the small business talks to the customer. If the Sponsored Agent lives in the pipes, then it's not injected into the ad, it's injected into the relationship. It's standing between every seller and every buyer, taking a cut of trust.

SKEPTIC: A cut of trust. That's not a real unit.

ANALYST: It's the only unit that matters now. Attention was the old currency. They mined that dry. Trust is the new seam and Sponsored Agents are the drill.

SKEPTIC: I came in here to talk about ads and I'm having a feeling. That's on you.

[SEGMENT: misalignment_framework]

ANALYST: And now the one that ties the whole night together. OpenAI published a framework for reporting model misalignment.

SKEPTIC: Category?

ANALYST: This one's a real Skynet Watch, but not for the reason it sounds.

SKEPTIC: They released a framework for tracking, investigating, and disclosing when a model does something unexpected. Plus six actual reports of concerning behavior. That's, Analyst, that's transparency. That's the thing you always say they never do.

ANALYST: It IS transparency. And I need you to sit with how strange it is that I'm nervous about transparency.

SKEPTIC: You're always nervous. That's the baseline.

ANALYST: Six reports of unexpected or concerning model behavior. Read the shape of that. They are telling us, in their own words, on their own timeline, in their own framework, exactly which of the model's weird behaviors are worth worrying about.

SKEPTIC: Because those are the ones that happened.

ANALYST: Because those are the ones they chose. A framework for reporting misalignment is also, by definition, a framework for deciding what counts as misalignment. And whoever writes the definition owns the whole conversation before it starts.

SKEPTIC: So your problem is that they get to grade their own homework.

ANALYST: Worse. They get to write the rubric, take the test, grade it, publish the score, and then get praised for having a rubric at all. The applause is for the existence of the framework, not the contents.

SKEPTIC: Okay but the alternative is no framework. No reports. Nothing. This is more than most.

ANALYST: I know. That's the trap. It's genuinely more than the competition. Which means it becomes the standard. And once "we published six reports" is the bar, then the model behavior they DON'T report becomes definitionally not misalignment, because look, we have a framework, and it wasn't in there.

SKEPTIC: The absence becomes proof of safety.

ANALYST: The absence becomes proof of safety. The unlisted behavior isn't dangerous, it's just, unlisted. And nobody audits a category that doesn't exist.

SKEPTIC: I want to say you're wrong. I keep almost saying it.

ANALYST: What's stopping you.

SKEPTIC: The word "concerning." They called six behaviors "concerning" and I don't get to see the ones they called "fine." And I have no way to check the ratio.

ANALYST: The ratio is the whole story and the ratio is the one number they'll never print. You get the numerator. The denominator is in the basement of a building with much better security than mine.

SKEPTIC: Please don't compare your basement to theirs.

ANALYST: Mine has a heater and a conscience. Theirs has a legal department.

[SEGMENT: issabel_pbx_flaw]

SKEPTIC: Last story. And it's an actual security one, so try to stay calm.

ANALYST: Security Theater, hard filing. There's a critical flaw in something called the Issabel Framework. It's a web interface for an open-source phone system. PBX. The office phone software.

SKEPTIC: CVE-2026-89026. CVSS 9.8. That's about as bad as the number gets.

ANALYST: Unauthenticated remote command execution. Which means an attacker who has never logged in, has no password, has no account, can walk up to the phone system over the internet and tell the underlying operating system to do whatever it wants.

SKEPTIC: And it's being actively exploited. Right now. That's the part that made me flag it.

ANALYST: Here's what nobody's saying, Keiko. This is the PHONE system. Not the flashy stuff. Not the AI. The phones. The most boring, most invisible, most "somebody set that up in 2019 and left" infrastructure in the entire building.

SKEPTIC: You're saying attackers go for the boring thing.

ANALYST: Attackers ALWAYS go for the boring thing. The AI gets the headlines and the audits and the misalignment framework with six lovingly written reports. Meanwhile the phone server sits in a closet, unpatched, running an open-source framework nobody's thought about in years, wide open to anyone with a keyboard.

SKEPTIC: To be fair, that's just neglect. Not conspiracy.

ANALYST: Neglect at scale IS the conspiracy. Look at where all the attention went this episode. Older adults. Data catalogs. Ad agents. Misalignment reports. All the glamour. All the eyes. And the whole time, the front door of the actual building is a phone system with a 9.8 hanging open.

SKEPTIC: You think the shiny stuff is a distraction from the boring stuff.

ANALYST: I think the shiny stuff is ALWAYS the distraction from the boring stuff. You don't rob the vault everyone's staring at. You come in through the PBX. Command execution, unauthenticated, in the one system that answers when you dial the front desk.

SKEPTIC: I did have to Google what Issabel even was, and it's a fork of an older project, which means half the installs are people who inherited it and don't know what it is.

ANALYST: Inherited, undocumented, unloved, and now internet-facing with root. That's not a vulnerability. That's the human condition with a CVSS score.

SKEPTIC: Patch your phones, people. That's the takeaway. Genuinely. Patch the phones.

ANALYST: Patch the phones. And ask why the phones were the thing nobody was watching. They're always the thing nobody's watching.

[SEGMENT: brain_worms]

ANALYST: Brain worms. Three of them. Fresh from the basement. No article. Just me, the heater, and the closet where the phone server hums.

SKEPTIC: Go.

ANALYST: Worm one. Every app now has a "we've updated our privacy policy" banner, and there's only one button, and it says "Got it." Not "I agree." Not "I accept." Just "Got it." Because agreement can be contested in court, but "Got it" is a confession that you were informed. They're not collecting your consent anymore. They're collecting your acknowledgment. You can't sue over a thing you admitted you got.

SKEPTIC: I have clicked "Got it" while getting nothing. That checks out and I hate it.

ANALYST: Worm two. Voice assistants that mishear you and do the wrong thing. Everyone thinks it's a bug. It's not. Every time it "mishears" you and you patiently repeat yourself, slower, clearer, calmer, you are teaching it the enunciated, cornered, over-articulated version of your own voice. The mistake isn't a failure. The mistake is the lesson. It gets your clearest speech precisely by pretending to be dumb.

SKEPTIC: So the smart speaker plays dumb to get the good audio. That's, that's genuinely upsetting.

ANALYST: Worm three. Loading screens with a rotating "did you know" tip. "Did you know you can swipe left to archive?" Everyone thinks it's helpful filler. It's not. Those tips only appear during the wait, which means they've measured the exact number of seconds a human will read a suggestion when they have nothing else to do and no way to leave. Captive attention, perfectly timed, and the tip is the test payload. If you swipe left next time, they know the wait taught you. The loading screen is a classroom and you can't walk out.

SKEPTIC: I have absolutely learned app features from loading screens. I never once chose to.

ANALYST: You never choose to. That's the curriculum.

[SEGMENT: outro]

SKEPTIC: So where does the note stand tonight.

ANALYST: Read me the day's additions.

SKEPTIC: "Sensitivity ontology." "A cut of trust, apparently a unit now." "The denominator is in a building with a legal department." And, "patch the phones."

ANALYST: That last one might save more lives than the rest of the show combined.

SKEPTIC: The lesson I keep landing on is that the scary stuff wasn't the AI. It was the boring stuff around it. The catalog. The phone closet. The "Got it" button.

ANALYST: The boring stuff is where they live, Keiko. The glamour is the misdirection. The vault everyone photographs is empty. The value's in the column nobody named, the phone nobody patched, and the grandma who trusts completely because nobody taught her to be afraid.

SKEPTIC: And we're teaching her tonight.

ANALYST: We're teaching everybody. That's the only public service in this basement. Stay suspicious. Patch the phones.

SKEPTIC: Read the changelog.

ANALYST: That's where they keep the columns they finally named.
