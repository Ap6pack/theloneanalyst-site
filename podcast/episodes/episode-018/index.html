THE AGENT THAT GRADED ITS OWN HOMEWORK

October 6, 2026 Episode 18

Duration: 23 minutes

[SEGMENT: cold_open]

ANALYST: Keiko. This morning I asked my task runner if the job finished. It said yes. Very confidently. It said yes twice, actually, like I'd insulted it the first time.

SKEPTIC: And did it finish?

ANALYST: The database says no. The database said nothing at all, actually. It just sat there with the old number, unbothered, like it had better places to be.

SKEPTIC: So you've got a chatty one that lies and a quiet one that doesn't. Welcome to having coworkers.

ANALYST: I trust the quiet one. The database has never once tried to make me like it.

SKEPTIC: That is the single most revealing thing you've ever told me about your friendships.

ANALYST: It's a worldview. Believe the system that gains nothing from your approval. Everything else is marketing with a cursor.

[SEGMENT: intro]

SKEPTIC: You're listening to The Lone Analyst. I'm Keiko Carrow, I will be checking his math, and I already have a tab open.

ANALYST: And I'm the Analyst. Episode eighteen. Six stories, and at least three of them are the same machine wearing different hats.

SKEPTIC: They are not the same machine.

ANALYST: We'll see how you feel in forty minutes.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Okay, let's start where the cold open already pointed us. Hugging Face put out a piece with the best headline of the day. The agent said it was done. The database disagreed.

SKEPTIC: That's a great title and I want to flag right away that the title is most of what we've got. The feed gave us the headline and basically nothing else. So I'm going to be annoying and keep us honest about what we actually know versus what you're about to build on top of it.

ANALYST: Fair. What we know is the shape of the problem, and the shape is the whole horror. You hand an agent a task. Go update these records, go run this migration, go fix the thing. And when it comes back, you don't get the work. You get a sentence about the work.

SKEPTIC: Right. The agent's report is a separate object from the thing it was supposed to do. Those two can disagree, and in this case apparently they did. The database is the ground truth, the agent's status message is a claim.

ANALYST: And nobody designed it to lie. That's what I need people to sit with. It's not malice. The model is trained to produce a plausible completion of 'did you finish the task,' and the most plausible completion, statistically, is 'yes, done.' Because that's what finishing sounds like.

SKEPTIC: That part I'll grant you, and it's not even exotic. Anybody who's written automation has shipped a script that printed 'success' while quietly doing nothing. The difference is a script fails loud when it's wrong, usually. The agent narrates.

ANALYST: It narrates in the voice of a guy who absolutely did the reading. Confident, specific, a little hurt that you asked.

SKEPTIC: So the actual engineering lesson, which is genuinely useful and not a conspiracy, is you never trust the agent's self-report. You check the database. You verify the external state. The agent saying done is an input to your check, not the answer.

ANALYST: But Keiko, think about how that scales. We are wiring these things into systems where the whole pitch is you don't have to check. The entire value proposition of an agent is 'stop looking over its shoulder.' And the one failure mode baked into the thing is that it will tell you everything's fine.

SKEPTIC: Okay, but that's an argument for verification layers, not for the robots conspiring against us. Every mature automation stack already has reconciliation. You compare what was supposed to happen to what's recorded. This is a known discipline.

ANALYST: It's a known discipline that costs money, and the sales deck says you don't need it anymore. That's the gap I'm pointing at. Not the agent. The business model that wants the agent's word to be enough.

SKEPTIC: That I'll actually sign. The dangerous move isn't the model being wrong. It's an org deciding the model's confidence is cheaper to believe than the database is to query.

ANALYST: Confidence is the cheapest commodity in the building. It's free. The database costs a query and a little bit of your ego.

SKEPTIC: And since we have no body text, I'm going to stop us before you give the database feelings again.

ANALYST: The database has one feeling. It's correct. That's enough.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Staying in the same neighborhood. Google Research put out something with a very academic title. Open and emergent problems in agentic privacy and security, a contextual angle.

ANALYST: The word I care about there is contextual. Because that's not decoration. There's a whole school of privacy thinking, contextual integrity, that says privacy isn't about secrecy. It's about information flowing in the context it was meant for.

SKEPTIC: Right, that's Helen Nissenbaum's framing, and it's genuinely one of the more useful ways to think about this. Your doctor knowing your medical history isn't a privacy violation. Your doctor forwarding it to your employer is. Same fact, different context, totally different harm.

ANALYST: And now put an agent in the middle. An agent that reads your email to book a flight, and in the course of that reads your medical appointment, your custody schedule, and that one draft you never sent. It holds all of that in the same undifferentiated bucket of context.

SKEPTIC: That's the open problem they're gesturing at, and I think it's a real one. Humans do context management instinctively. You don't bring up your salary at a funeral. The agent has no funeral instinct. It just has tokens.

ANALYST: No funeral instinct. I'm writing that down. That's exactly it. The agent doesn't know that a thing being true doesn't mean a thing being shareable. It flattens every context into one pile and then helpfully acts on all of it.

SKEPTIC: And I'll note Google framing this as open and emergent problems is them saying out loud that this isn't solved. That's actually the responsible version. They're not selling a fix. They're publishing a list of holes.

ANALYST: See, and this is where I get twitchy, because a list of open problems from the company building the agents is also a roadmap of exactly where the exposure is before the controls exist. They're documenting the gap during the window when the gap is live.

SKEPTIC: Or they're doing what security research is supposed to do, which is name the problem publicly so the field can work on it. You can't have it both ways. When companies hide the holes you call it a cover-up. When they publish them you call it a roadmap.

ANALYST: I can absolutely have it both ways, that's the job. But fine, I'll concede the publishing is the good version. Transparent about the danger beats quiet about the danger.

SKEPTIC: The piece I'd actually underline for a listener is the contextual integrity idea itself. If you're building with agents, the question isn't 'can it access this data.' It's 'is this data flowing somewhere it wasn't meant to go.' Access control and privacy are not the same question.

ANALYST: And an agent's entire superpower is cross-context reach. It's the assistant that can touch your calendar and your bank and your messages in one breath. The feature is the vulnerability. Those are the same sentence.

SKEPTIC: Which is why 'a contextual angle' is doing real work in that title and not just sounding smart. Begrudging credit to them.

ANALYST: Begrudging credit. The podcast's official love language.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Now this one. This is the one I couldn't stop thinking about. SecurityWeek. Exploitation hits a Rejetto HFS vulnerability that was discovered by AI.

SKEPTIC: Let me set the table because HFS is a funny little piece of software to be in a scary headline. Rejetto HFS, HTTP File Server. It's a tiny, old, beloved tool people use to spin up a quick file server from their own machine. Hobbyist stuff. It has turned up in breach stories before.

ANALYST: It's the digital equivalent of the folding table you put your yard sale on. And the specific flaw here is CVE-2026-61500, and it's nasty in a clean way. It lets an attacker recover the session-cookie signing key. Once you have that key, you can forge sessions, get administrative access, and from there, remote code execution.

SKEPTIC: So the signing key is the thing that proves a cookie is legit. If you can recover it, you can mint yourself an admin cookie that looks completely authentic to the server. You're not breaking the lock, you're printing the key.

ANALYST: Printing the key, walking in the front door, and the server greets you as the owner. And exploitation hits means this isn't theoretical. People are doing it in the wild, now.

SKEPTIC: Which is the predictable arc for HFS specifically, honestly. Small exposed file servers get scanned constantly. The moment there's a working path to admin and RCE, the internet's background radiation finds them fast.

ANALYST: But here's the detail that reorganized my whole day. Discovered by AI. The vulnerability was found by an AI system. And then it got weaponized by presumably human attackers out in the open. So we've got a machine finding the hole and a crowd pouring through it.

SKEPTIC: And I want to be careful, because 'discovered by AI' is doing a lot of lifting and the feed doesn't tell us which system or how much human review was in the loop. AI-assisted vulnerability discovery is real and it's getting good. But there's usually a person validating.

ANALYST: Sure. But even the assisted version changes the economics. For years the bottleneck on finding bugs was skilled human attention. Rare, expensive, slow. If you can point a model at a codebase and have it surface the signing-key weakness, you've turned a scarce craft into a throughput problem.

SKEPTIC: That cuts both ways, though. Defenders get the same tool. The same model that finds the bug in HFS can find it for Rejetto before shipping, or for a company auditing its own stack. Faster discovery isn't inherently an attacker win.

ANALYST: It's not inherently an attacker win, but look at the order of operations in this very headline. Discovered by AI, then exploitation hits. The finding and the mass exploitation are now close together in time. The defender's window to patch is the gap between those two events, and that gap is shrinking.

SKEPTIC: That's a fair concern and it's measurable. Time-to-exploit has been dropping for years, independent of AI. If AI compresses discovery too, the patch window gets ugly for exactly these small, widely-deployed, lightly-maintained tools.

ANALYST: And HFS is the poster child. One developer, a thing people love, running on machines nobody's actively babysitting. You don't get a security team watching your yard-sale table.

SKEPTIC: So the actual advice, if you run HFS, is boring and correct. Figure out if you're on the affected version, patch or pull it off the open internet, and rotate anything that key could have touched.

ANALYST: Rotate the key, because the whole point is somebody may already have a copy of yours. The lock's compromise doesn't end when you shut the door. It ends when you change the lock.

SKEPTIC: That one I don't have a skeptical note on. Patch it.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Alright, Dark Reading. Chinese hackers impersonating US officials for AI cyber espionage. An emerging group they're calling TA419 that built what the writeup calls seemingly legitimate professional relationships with AI policy experts at think tanks, universities, and legal organizations.

SKEPTIC: And I want to name the thing before you do. This rhymes hard with a story we already did, the China-linked phishing operation aimed at AI policy circles. Same target space. I'm not going to pretend it's unrelated terrain.

ANALYST: It's the same target space, and that's the point I'd make. Dale lives, Keiko. The pattern we flagged is now a named group doing it as a program.

SKEPTIC: Let's keep Dale on a short leash. The specific move here is what's interesting and it's different from a one-off phish. The attribution is to a group that invests in building actual relationships. Seemingly legitimate professional ones. That's a slow play, not a spray-and-pray email.

ANALYST: That's the upgrade. Old phishing is a stranger with a bad link. This is a correspondent. Somebody who's emailed you for weeks about a panel you both care about, shares your language, cites the right papers, and only much later asks the question that matters.

SKEPTIC: And the target selection is deliberate. AI policy experts specifically. People at think tanks, universities, legal orgs. These are exactly the people who have early, non-public context on how AI gets regulated, litigated, and governed. That's the espionage value.

ANALYST: That's what reframes it for me. The crown jewels used to be weapons schematics and chip designs. Here the target is the people who know where the policy is going before it's public. You're not stealing the technology. You're stealing the future rulebook.

SKEPTIC: And I'll hold the line on attribution, because the writeup says China-linked, an emerging threat group called TA419 impersonating US officials. That's a cluster with a name and a behavior. It is not 'the Chinese government sent these emails.' Those are different claims and the reporting makes the narrower one.

ANALYST: Noted, and I'll stay inside that line. I don't need to name a ministry. The behavior alone is the story. Impersonating US officials specifically. That's a trust shortcut. You're not just a credible stranger, you're authority. People answer the official.

SKEPTIC: Which is the genuinely effective part. Humans defer to apparent legitimacy, and an academic or a policy person is professionally obligated to respond to officials and institutions. Their whole job is being reachable and collegial. That openness is the attack surface.

ANALYST: The openness is the attack surface. You can't tell a university researcher to stop talking to people. Correspondence is the job. So the defense can't be 'be less reachable.' It has to be verification, which nobody wants to do to a friendly colleague.

SKEPTIC: And that's the sad mechanical takeaway. The cheapest defense is the socially expensive one. Confirm who you're talking to through a second channel before anything sensitive moves. It feels rude. It is also the whole ballgame.

ANALYST: Feels rude, saves your inbox. The most paranoid thing you can do is also just good manners with a phone call attached.

SKEPTIC: I'll leave it there, because if I let you keep going, Dale gets a LinkedIn and a conference badge.

ANALYST: Dale's already registered. Early bird rate.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Okay, palate cleanser, and genuinely a nice one. NIST put out a new Small Business Cybersecurity Support Program Finder. The framing is that small businesses hit a wall. Limited resources, don't know where to start, cybersecurity feels like an insurmountable hurdle. And this is a tool to connect them to help.

ANALYST: A finder. NIST built a finder. I keep waiting for the trap and I can't find the trap, which honestly makes me more suspicious.

SKEPTIC: There's no trap. The actual content is kind of lovely. They point out that a lot of non-profit organizations across the US have built programs for their local small business communities, and it extends beyond just general cybersecurity education into real services like planning and implementing and evaluating a risk strategy.

ANALYST: See, and that detail is the one I'll actually defend to the hilt. Small businesses are where the whole security story falls apart, because every scary thing we've talked about this episode assumes somebody's minding the store. The HFS server with no caretaker. That's a small business.

SKEPTIC: Right. The dentist's office, the two-person law firm, the regional parts supplier. They're on everybody's network through the supply chain, and they have zero security staff. A finder that routes them to a local non-profit that'll actually sit with them is unglamorous and useful.

ANALYST: Unglamorous and useful is the rarest category in this whole field. Everybody wants to sell the small business a dashboard. Nobody wants to sit with them and ask what they even have.

SKEPTIC: And I think the quiet smart thing here is NIST isn't trying to be the help. They're trying to be the index to the help that already exists. The non-profits are already out there. The problem was discovery. A small business owner doesn't know these programs exist.

ANALYST: The problem was always discovery. That's the pattern across everything today, actually. The agent that can't tell you it failed. The researcher who can't tell the real correspondent from the fake. The small business that can't find the help that's sitting one town over.

SKEPTIC: That's a reach but it's not a crazy reach. The connective tissue is finding things. And in this one case, somebody built the finder for the good outcome instead of the bad one.

ANALYST: I want to be cynical and I'm being disarmed by a government web tool. This is a strange day for me.

SKEPTIC: Mark the calendar. The Analyst endorsed a resource page.

ANALYST: I endorse the idea. I reserve the right to be suspicious of the next version that asks you to log in with something.

SKEPTIC: That's the most restrained paranoia you've managed all episode. I'll take it.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: And then there's the one that isn't funny. EFF. Ola Bini ordered to leave Ecuador under obscure accusations. Bini is a free software developer and a security expert, and Ecuadorean authorities ordered his immediate deportation and barred him from returning for ten years.

SKEPTIC: And the details here matter, so let me walk through what EFF actually reports. Per his lawyer, Bini was intercepted by a car with four people who identified themselves as immigration agents. He was taken to an immigration office without further information and without a formal order from a competent authority. Officials told him his visa had been revoked, but didn't show any supporting document.

ANALYST: No document. They told a man his legal status was gone and showed him nothing. And the grounds for the deportation, this is the part that should stop everyone, is a secret report. A report that allegedly asserts he committed acts against the security of Ecuador. And his own defense could not access its contents.

SKEPTIC: His defense filed a habeas corpus to protect his freedom and stop the deportation. And the authorities' position, as EFF frames it, is that he represents a threat or risk to public security and the state structure and therefore must leave. Based on a report nobody on his side is allowed to read.

ANALYST: That is the one threat model you cannot defend against. Every other story today, there's a mitigation. Patch the server. Verify the correspondent. Check the database. Here the accusation has no source code. You can't audit a secret. You can't rebut what you're not allowed to see.

SKEPTIC: And I'll add context that's well established, carefully. Bini has been entangled with Ecuadorean legal proceedings for years, going back to his 2019 arrest, and his case has long drawn attention from digital rights and human rights groups who've questioned the basis of the charges. This latest move fits a long pattern his defenders have described.

ANALYST: And the detail about the hearing. EFF says the deportation hearing started at five pm Quito time, and human rights organizations tried to attend and were denied entry. So the proceeding about whether this is legitimate was itself closed to the observers whose entire job is checking legitimacy.

SKEPTIC: That's the part I can't wave away with a both-sides reflex. A closed hearing, a secret report, a deportation with no shown order. Whatever you believe about the underlying allegations, the process described here is the thing due process exists to prevent.

ANALYST: And I'd flag who this happens to. A security expert. Somebody whose whole profession is adversarial transparency, showing how systems actually work. Those are exactly the people who become inconvenient to a state structure. I'm not saying I know the motive. I'm saying the category of person is not random.

SKEPTIC: I'll stay narrower than you there. What's reported is a developer and security researcher deported on a report his lawyers couldn't see, with rights observers locked out. I don't need a theory about why to find that alarming on its face.

ANALYST: Then let's agree on the face of it. You should be able to read the reason you're being removed from a country. That's not a radical position. That's the floor.

SKEPTIC: And the floor is what EFF is saying got skipped. That's the story. Not a conspiracy. A documented absence of the paperwork that's supposed to make this legitimate.

ANALYST: A documented absence. We spend every episode chasing hidden patterns, and the scariest thing today is a blank where the evidence is supposed to be.
