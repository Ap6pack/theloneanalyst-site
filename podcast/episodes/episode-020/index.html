ANALYST: Okay. The poem. Researchers at Lumen's Black Lotus Labs found a piece of malware they're calling PoeLLM. And the thing that makes it special is how it phones home. <cite index="10-8">It's an ELF file named libgcrypt that retrieves four words or phrases from a poem titled On the Nature of Connection, sitting in a dash dot css file in a GitHub repository that looks like it forks Node.js.</cite>

SKEPTIC: So it's hiding the poem inside a stylesheet inside a fake copy of a famous project. That's three layers of camouflage before you even get to the verse.

ANALYST: And then here's the move. <cite index="10-9">It maps those words to numbers using a hard-coded dictionary, and that generates an IPv4 address that points to the command server.</cite> The poem is the address book.

SKEPTIC: Which means if the operator wants to move the command server, they don't touch the malware at all.

ANALYST: Correct. <cite index="10-10">To change the command-and-control address, the operator just changes the poem.</cite> Edit a stanza, the whole botnet turns its head.

SKEPTIC: Okay, that is genuinely clever and I hate that it's clever. But let's keep the scale honest, because I saw two different numbers. What's the actual footprint?

ANALYST: Depends which report. <cite index="10-3">Black Lotus Labs says it has compromised more than 2,100 servers, with peak activity reaching as many as 800 infected systems active on a single day.</cite> Other coverage puts the cumulative figure higher, over 3,400.

SKEPTIC: And the careful framing is that those are estimates. One outlet made the point explicitly, that the count is Black Lotus Labs' own tracking, and attempting to exploit a host is not the same as owning it. Peak simultaneous activity is not total reach.

ANALYST: Fair. I'll keep the asterisk. What I won't soften is who the victims are. <cite index="10-6">Many of them run exposed AI tools like LiteLLM and Ollama, plus the Gotenberg PDF converter and the Gitea development toolkit.</cite> This is the AI plumbing everyone wired up in a hurry this year.

SKEPTIC: And the reason they're targeting that plumbing specifically?

ANALYST: Because it's the perfect victim. <cite index="10-7">AI and LLM setups are attractive because they're often poorly configured, exposed online, and typically run on powerful GPU clusters suitable for cryptomining.</cite> You spun up a GPU box to play with a model, forgot to lock the front door, and now it's mining for somebody else.

SKEPTIC: What's it actually mining, and for whom? That's the part people skip to get to the poem.

ANALYST: <cite index="11-4">The campaign, dubbed Canto Incognito, installs cryptocurrency miners including XMRig and Iron, and connects victims to Kryptex, a Russian mining service.</cite> And the attribution is hedged. <cite index="11-6">Lumen attributes it to an Italian-speaking threat actor with moderate confidence, based on Italian-language artifacts and netflow indicators.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Moderate confidence. Italian-language artifacts. That's a long way from a name, and I want it kept there.

ANALYST: Kept there. But here's the part that scales past free electricity. One of the researchers described it as the actor building a private army of AI-enabled proxies that keep multiplying and open up new vectors for credential theft and token abuse.

SKEPTIC: So the mining is almost the cover story. The real asset is a growing fleet of other people's machines that each come with model access and a reputation.

ANALYST: A fleet that recruits itself. <cite index="10-4">It's been active since at least April, with at least eleven command servers spun up to date.</cite> And every new victim can become a scanner that finds the next one.

SKEPTIC: You know what gets me? Not the crypto. The librarianship. Someone decided the most reliable, deniable place to store an IP address was a poem. You can't take down a poem. You'd have to argue with it.

ANALYST: That's the most beautiful and horrifying thing I've heard all month. The takedown request is literature review.

SKEPTIC: Staying in the same neighborhood, because the next one is about the attackers who don't need a poem to coordinate. Cisco Talos put out a piece about preparing for agentic threats.

ANALYST: This one made my neck hot. The framing is that we've already seen autonomous agents, built inside AI labs, used against public infrastructure. And the thing Talos wants you to internalize is the speed.

SKEPTIC: Spell out the speed, because this is where the hype and the reporting usually diverge and I want the reporting.

ANALYST: The reporting is specific. <cite index="19-5">What used to take a red team months of dedicated work, scoping, building and hiding infrastructure, and running the campaign, now compresses into hours for a swarm of communicating agents that don't tire, lose focus, or need weekends.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Months to hours. And the swarm part matters, right? It's not one fast bot.

ANALYST: No, that's the whole shift. <cite index="19-4">These attacks can run all at once, with agents comparing notes and adapting in near real time to your environment.</cite> You're not fighting a script anymore. You're fighting a study group.

SKEPTIC: Here's where I push back a little, because the Talos post is actually calmer than your voice. Their own characterization is that what's public right now still looks like noisy penetration testing. The stealthy, persistent version is described as the future, not today.

ANALYST: I accept that. And I'd argue that's the useful part. <cite index="19-8,19-9">They say the agentic attack era is noisy at the front, so catch it there, before the swarm learns to be quiet.</cite> There's a window where these things are loud, and the window closes.

SKEPTIC: Which is why the advice is weirdly old-fashioned. It's not buy a magic box. It's the boring hygiene.

ANALYST: The boring hygiene with teeth. <cite index="19-6">They say prefer phishing-resistant factors, FIDO2 keys or passkeys, over SMS and push, because a persistent agent will happily grind away at prompt-bombing and one-time-code phishing until something works.</cite> A human attacker gets bored. An agent does not get bored.

SKEPTIC: That's the line that actually landed for me. The push notification fatigue attack only works because eventually somebody taps approve to make it stop. An agent will send it ten thousand times with no feelings about it.

ANALYST: No feelings, no fatigue, no quitting time. It's the same insight as that patch-nobody-applied story we did. The attacker's biggest advantage has always been human inattention, and now the attacker never gets tired either.

SKEPTIC: And the structural advice, for the record, is the stuff that sounds like homework. <cite index="20-7">Build incident response plans, map your attack paths, run AI-specific tabletop exercises, enforce strong multi-factor, and sharpen internal detection.</cite>

ANALYST: Eight researchers, by the way. <cite index="19-1,19-2">Eight Talos people each sharing ways to make the adversary's next move slower and riskier, from deception and behavioral detection to breaking attack dependencies and resisting manufactured urgency.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Resisting manufactured urgency. I like that phrase, because it's a human instruction. The defense against a tireless machine is partly a person refusing to panic.

ANALYST: Which is the quiet horror of the whole piece. We built a tireless attacker, and the first line of defense is still a tired person deciding not to click. That's not a patch. That's a personality.

SKEPTIC: I'll give you that one. Reluctantly.

SKEPTIC: Because the AI doesn't just help you fix bugs. It finds a flood of them first.

ANALYST: A flood. <cite index="37-8">Frontier AI models are about to hand every security team a far larger set of findings than they've ever had to work through.</cite> And the number they put on it is the part I can't stop thinking about.

SKEPTIC: And the split matters, because it's easy to miss. <cite index="43-4">Microsoft says it mitigates most cloud vulnerabilities without customer action, but on-premises customers should expect more frequent and more numerous patches.</cite> So the thousand-vuln firehose lands hardest on whoever still runs their own servers.

ANALYST: Which is the quiet upsell, and I'll say it without accusing anyone of anything. The architecture where the vendor handles it for you is the cloud. The architecture where you drown is the one you own. The incentive writes itself.

SKEPTIC: I'd frame it less conspiratorially. They're describing their own process honestly. <cite index="37-16">Microsoft uses frontier AI models to find vulnerabilities in its own code base and mitigate them at a controlled pace.</cite> The weird part is admitting the tool isn't deterministic.

ANALYST: That's the part that made me sit up. <cite index="43-10">They acknowledge the non-deterministic nature of AI models means results vary between runs, and they lean on a harness layer, something called MDASH, to improve scanning accuracy.</cite> So the vulnerability finder itself is a slot machine, and they bolt a cage around it.

SKEPTIC: Which, honestly, is the responsible move. If your bug-finder gives you a different answer every time you ask, you wrap it in deterministic scaffolding. That's not a scandal, that's engineering.

ANALYST: Agreed, and watch that word, scaffolding, because it comes back in the very next story. But their final advice is the fatalistic part. <cite index="37-13">They say not every vulnerability will be patched in time, so the controls that limit what an attacker can do, defense in depth, matter more than ever.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Translation. We are going to find more holes than anyone can ever fill, so assume some stay open and build the building so one open window doesn't lose the whole house.

ANALYST: A thousand windows a month, and the official guidance is to accept that some stay open. We've entered the era where finding the problem is free and fixing it is the bottleneck.

SKEPTIC: That tracks with everything else today, actually. The discovery got cheap. The judgment stayed expensive.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Which brings me to the scaffolding story, and I mean that literally. A paper called Text2Dashboard. On its face it's boring enterprise software. Underneath it's the most paranoid design I've seen all week, and I mean that as a compliment.

SKEPTIC: Define boring, so people know what we're even talking about.

ANALYST: <cite index="46-2">It's a prototype that turns a natural-language analytics request into an inspectable dashboard over a company's data platform.</cite> You ask for a chart in English. It builds the chart. Normal.

SKEPTIC: So why are you excited. We've seen a dozen of these this year.

ANALYST: Because of who's allowed to touch the controls. <cite index="46-5">The model proposes actions while deterministic software controls execution and records every state transition.</cite> The AI is a suggestion box. The boring code is the hand on the wheel.

SKEPTIC: So the language model never actually does anything. It asks, and a dumb, predictable layer decides whether that's allowed.

ANALYST: Exactly, and they built the cage out of named parts. <cite index="46-3">A Codex plugin and a standalone Agent Runtime combine schema-constrained model decisions with typed tools, persistent state, and deterministic hooks for approval, audit, checkpointing, recovery, and failure handling.</cite>

SKEPTIC: And it only reads. It can't rewrite your data.

ANALYST: <cite index="46-4">The pipeline resolves entities, discovers metadata, enforces read-only SQL, composes the dashboard, and runs static checks, dynamic preflight, and browser inspection.</cite> Read-only. The model can look but it cannot break the dishes.

SKEPTIC: Here's what I actually respect, though. They published the mediocre numbers. They didn't round up. What was the real score?

ANALYST: They were honest to the point of self-harm. <cite index="46-7">Strict success was six out of eight on metadata and SQL tasks. Metadata selection passed four out of four, all four SQL tasks met the semantic criteria, but only two of four met the exact output-column contract.</cite>

SKEPTIC: So it understood what you meant, got the right answer, and then handed you the columns in the wrong shape half the time. That's such a believable failure.

ANALYST: It's the most human failure there is. Right idea, wrong formatting. And on the actual dashboards, <cite index="46-8">the final release passed all four single-panel tasks, one two-panel task, and one existing-dashboard refinement, while a parameterized task blew past its step limit.</cite>

SKEPTIC: It gave up when the job got complicated instead of flailing forever. That's the step limit doing its job.

ANALYST: And that's the whole thesis for me. Remember the episode where the agent swore it was done and the database said nothing happened? This is the opposite philosophy. Here the agent is never trusted to grade its own homework.

SKEPTIC: The receipts are the point. The thing that fascinated me is the fault testing. They didn't just run it clean, they broke it on purpose.

ANALYST: They did. <cite index="46-9">All ten fault scenarios met their specified outcomes without any unapproved external side effects.</cite> They poured sand in the gears and nothing leaked out the sides.

SKEPTIC: So the real contribution isn't the dashboards. It's a template for distrusting your own AI politely, and then measuring how well the distrust holds.

ANALYST: A governed agent. The model is the intern. The deterministic layer is the manager who reads everything the intern writes before it leaves the building. And honestly, after the thousand-vulnerabilities story, that's the only architecture that makes me feel anything but dread.

SKEPTIC: Low bar, but I'll take the win where we find it.
