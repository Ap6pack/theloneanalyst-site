[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Fine, calmer, your Gary story but with data. There's a report out this week, the one driving the whole "velocity paradox" headline.

ANALYST: Here's the number that does it to you. <cite index="2-2">79% of organizations are already running AI agents in production, yet only 2% use purpose-built identity security tools to govern them.</cite> Two percent, Keiko.

SKEPTIC: And I want to flag the source before you build a cathedral on it. This is SailPoint's own report, their fifth annual Horizons of Identity Security. SailPoint sells exactly the kind of tooling the report says everyone is missing. <cite index="9-1">They frame it as a 40-to-1 gap between AI adoption and identity security readiness.</cite>

ANALYST: Totally fair. Vendor publishes report, report finds the world urgently needs the vendor. Noted. But the shape of the thing holds up even if you discount the sales pitch.

SKEPTIC: It kind of does, annoyingly. The part I couldn't argue with is the split between human and machine. <cite index="9-3">On human identity, organizations nearly halved those at the lowest maturity level, from 45% in 2022 to about 23% today.</cite>

ANALYST: Fifteen years of people getting yelled at about passwords. And then the agents show up. <cite index="3-2,9-4">For agent identity, 54% of organizations are still sitting at that bottom rung, Horizon 1.</cite> That's a worse starting line than humans had five years ago.

SKEPTIC: So the race car arrives before anyone's built the track.

ANALYST: And the agents don't drive like us. The report's own line is the whole thing: <cite index="2-7">"Identity architectures built for humans cannot govern autonomous agents executing thousands of transactions per second."</cite> You cannot do a quarterly access review on something that acts a thousand times a second.

SKEPTIC: Here's where I get genuinely uneasy, and it's not the conspiracy, it's the operations. <cite index="3-8">Only 15% of them can provision non-human access in real time, but 80% of leaders think their tooling gap is moderate or smaller.</cite> So the people in charge think they're mostly fine.

ANALYST: That's the pattern that actually scares me more than any breach. It's not that the door's unlocked. It's that everybody's confident the door is locked. <cite index="3-5">And these agents are already 22% of all non-human accounts, while 85% of orgs lean on legacy identity tools that were never built for them.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Okay but let me push back on your doom a little. An agent isn't a rogue intelligence. It's a service account with a fancier haircut. We've had non-human identities forever. Machines logging into machines.

ANALYST: Right, except the old service account did one boring thing on a schedule. The agent improvises. It chains tools together, it decides mid-task to go email Gary. The old machine account never surprised you. That's the actual change.

SKEPTIC: That I'll grant. The surprise is the new part. A thing with standing access and initiative is a different animal than a cron job.

ANALYST: And nobody owns it. That's the phrase that stuck in my head reading this. Every agent nobody put into the identity program becomes access that nobody owns. An orphan with keys.

SKEPTIC: I'll leave the sales pitch where it belongs, but I'm writing down "orphan with keys." That one's real whether or not SailPoint sells the orphanage.

ANALYST: Okay. Now you let me have Prometheus.

SKEPTIC: Go. But facts only, because this is a live federal case and the man has pleaded not guilty.

ANALYST: Noted, allegedly, all of it. <cite index="14-1,17-4">Anibal Alexander Canelon Aguirre, fifty years old, went by Prometheus and "The Engineer."</cite> And here's the one that made me sit up: <cite index="16-7,16-8">the FBI added him to its Ten Most Wanted Fugitives list back in March, making him the 540th person ever on it and the first cybercriminal to make the list.</cite>

SKEPTIC: That's the detail that's actually historic. The Ten Most Wanted list is decades old and it's always been bank robbers, mob guys, fugitives with guns. He's the first one who got there with code.

ANALYST: Code with a specialty. <cite index="17-6,17-7">Prosecutors say he developed malware called Ploutus, used in what's called ATM jackpotting, where you install software that forces the cash machine to spit out all its money without debiting anyone's account.</cite>

SKEPTIC: So nobody's card gets charged. The machine just empties itself like it's possessed.

ANALYST: And it's smarter than that. <cite index="19-2,19-3">According to the FBI he could remotely check how much cash any infected ATM was holding, and that's how they'd decide which machines were worth hitting.</cite> It's inventory management. For crime.

SKEPTIC: Okay, that's the part that actually impressed me, and I hate that it did. That's not smash-and-grab. That's a dashboard.

ANALYST: And then the part that sells the name. <cite index="14-6">The software had protections to make it hard for investigators to analyze, and it could delete itself off the ATM to hide evidence of the attack.</cite> He steals the fire and then erases the match.

SKEPTIC: Let me put the scale on the table, because you'll underplay it to make it spooky and it's spooky enough straight. <cite index="19-4">The FBI estimates roughly $61 million taken from ATMs since 2021, with about half the proceeds going to Tren de Aragua.</cite>

ANALYST: Which is the part that lifts this out of ordinary cybercrime. <cite index="16-1">Tren de Aragua is a Venezuelan gang the U.S. has designated a foreign terrorist organization.</cite> So this isn't a lone coder in a hoodie. It's an ATM turned into a funding pipe.

SKEPTIC: And the reach was not small. <cite index="14-7">The operation targeted or carried out attacks across 47 states, Washington D.C., and several foreign countries, according to the Justice Department.</cite> Forty-seven states. That's basically a national footprint.

ANALYST: Here's my actual theory, and it's not even that paranoid. We built a cash-dispensing robot, parked it on every corner, gave it a network connection, and then acted shocked that somebody wrote a program to talk to it. The ATM was always a jackpot machine. It just needed the right coins.

SKEPTIC: That's less a conspiracy and more a product review. Where he is now: <cite index="12-5,15-3">he was captured in Venezuela, brought back, and pleaded not guilty to four federal conspiracy charges in a Nebraska courtroom, and he's detained awaiting trial.</cite>

ANALYST: Nebraska. The man ran a continent-spanning malware operation and the reckoning happens in Nebraska. There's something perfect about that.

SKEPTIC: And the wider dragnet is huge. <cite index="12-11,15-4">The DOJ says nearly 350 alleged Tren de Aragua members and associates have been charged.</cite> He's the headline, but he's one node.

ANALYST: One node who named himself after a Titan. If you're going to build the self-deleting malware, I guess you don't go by Steve.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Staying in smuggling, but legal-gray instead of jackpot. There's a new Georgetown report asking whether we should literally track AI chips.

ANALYST: Georgetown's CSET, the Feldgoise, Miller, Dohmen report. And the number they open with is genuinely staggering. <cite index="30-9">One estimate says over 450,000 advanced AI chips were smuggled into China in 2024 and 2025 alone, which is one to two cutting-edge AI supercomputers' worth of compute.</cite>

SKEPTIC: To be clear about the baseline, <cite index="30-7,30-8">since 2022 the U.S. has restricted exports of the most advanced AI chips to China and a few other countries, and smugglers have repeatedly gotten around it, diverting hundreds of thousands of chips.</cite> So the controls exist. They just leak.

ANALYST: And the leak has a face. There's this image that's become the symbol of the whole thing. In a warehouse somewhere in Southeast Asia, <cite index="37-4,37-5">a woman in a sleeveless dress using a red hair dryer to peel the serial stickers off server packages, so the chips could be sneaked past the controls.</cite>

SKEPTIC: A hair dryer. That's the adversary to the most sophisticated export regime on earth. A twelve-dollar hair dryer and some patience.

ANALYST: Which is exactly why the proposed fix is so wild. The idea is location verification. Make the chip prove where it physically is. And the report says it can actually work, with caveats.

SKEPTIC: The caveats are the whole report, though. <cite index="36-1">They say these anti-diversion tools have significant shortcomings and wouldn't be foolproof or inexpensive.</cite> Nobody's claiming a magic fence.

ANALYST: But here's the method I can't stop thinking about. <cite index="38-1,38-3">Ping-based location verification, where you measure how long a signal takes to travel, and they modeled over ten million scenarios and found it detects more smuggled chips per dollar than physical inspections in basically every case.</cite> The chip phones home, and the lag tells you if it's lying about where it lives.

SKEPTIC: It's clever. Physics doesn't negotiate. You can peel every sticker off the box, but you can't talk the speed of light into saying you're in Arizona when you're in Shenzhen.

ANALYST: And that's where my brain goes to the dark place. We're about to build, with a straight face, a global system where every top-tier chip continuously reports its coordinates to an authority. We invented chip surveillance and we're calling it an export tool.

SKEPTIC: Okay, but read what they actually recommend before you crown the Shadow Board. <cite index="38-4">They suggest a hybrid: ping-based as the primary system, backed by targeted physical inspections, because the two methods deter different smuggling tricks.</cite> That's not a panopticon, that's a layered audit.

ANALYST: For now. The honest framing from the authors is modest, I'll give them that. <cite index="29-13">Their own line is that location verification could be deployed for a feasible but non-trivial cost and would enhance enforcement by generating leads and imposing costs on smugglers.</cite> Generating leads. Not catching everyone.

SKEPTIC: Which is the grown-up version of this. It's not a wall. It's friction. You make the hair dryer trick more expensive and more traceable, and some smugglers decide it's not worth it.

ANALYST: I just want it on the record that the most advanced technology on the planet is currently being defeated by a beauty appliance, and the counter-plan is to make the technology tattle on itself.

SKEPTIC: That's a fair summary and I'm not going to improve on it.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Speaking of money ending up where it wasn't supposed to go. The DNC is suing the president.

SKEPTIC: They are, and let me lay it out cleanly because the money trail is the whole story. <cite index="24-5,26-11">The Democratic National Committee sued this week over television ads promoting Trump that cost taxpayers millions, naming Trump, the White House, DHS, and the Office of Management and Budget as defendants.</cite>

ANALYST: And where did the money come from? This is the part that sounds invented. It came out of a budget line for memorials.

SKEPTIC: It genuinely did. <cite index="22-7,22-8">The complaint says OMB approved $20 million in Customs and Border Protection funds under a category for commemorative events, money that congressional materials say was meant for things like line-of-duty death memorials and agency anniversaries.</cite>

ANALYST: So the money set aside to honor officers who died in the line of duty got repurposed into campaign-style TV. I want to be careful, but that transfer is real and documented, right?

SKEPTIC: The reporting is specific on the mechanics. <cite index="59-4">Federal records show the money moved into that CBP commemorative-events account on September 19th, and the next day a $20 million national media campaign contract was awarded to a Maryland ad agency.</cite> Then the ads started.

ANALYST: Twenty-four hours from "memorial fund" to "national media campaign." That is a fast grief-to-advertising pipeline.

SKEPTIC: And the DNC's actual legal theory isn't vibes. <cite index="24-9">Legal experts say it runs afoul of a federal statute barring appropriated money from being used for publicity or propaganda purposes, and potentially other laws.</cite> That ban's been on the books a long time.

ANALYST: The White House's defense is the part I find almost artistic. They're calling these public service announcements.

SKEPTIC: They are. <cite index="24-11">The White House defends them as public service announcements, like what past administrations ran to promote policies.</cite> But here's the distinction the experts draw. <cite index="24-12">Analysts say these differ from normal PSAs because they aren't aimed at helping the public benefit from a specific government program.</cite>

ANALYST: Right, a real PSA tells you to get a flu shot or how to apply for disaster aid. It has a thing for you to do. What's the call to action here, feel good about the president?

SKEPTIC: Pretty much. <cite index="21-4,21-5">The lawsuit points out many of the ads mirror Republican midterm messaging, and one of the newest uses language identical to a 2024 Trump campaign ad.</cite> The old version had a campaign call to action. The new one swaps it for a disclosure that says the government paid for it.

ANALYST: So they took a campaign ad, deleted "vote for me," stamped "paid for by the U.S. Government" on it, and that's the magic spell that turns propaganda into a public service. Change six words, change the funding source.

SKEPTIC: And the timing is the aggressive part. <cite index="22-2,22-10">Election Day is less than a month away, so the DNC is asking a federal judge to step in before the rest of the $20 million gets spent.</cite>

ANALYST: How much is already gone?

SKEPTIC: <cite index="24-4">The ads began in September and have cost taxpayers at least $12 million of the contracted $20 million.</cite> And there's a wrinkle: Trump has said he'll shift future costs to his super PAC.

ANALYST: Which is a tell, right? If it was always legitimate government messaging, you wouldn't need to quietly move the bill to a political action committee the second people noticed.

SKEPTIC: I'd be careful with that leap, because "we'll pay going forward" could just be political cover rather than an admission. But I agree it's a strange thing to volunteer about a thing you insist is above board.

ANALYST: It's the memorial line that I can't let go of. Of every account in the entire federal budget, the one they reached into was the one meant to commemorate the dead. If you wrote that in a show, the editor would say it's too on the nose.

SKEPTIC: And the case will turn on dry statutory language, not on how on-the-nose it feels. But yeah. The budget line is the budget line.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Let's climb out of the gutter and into the ivory tower. MIT ran one of their 3 Questions pieces on where AI goes in academia.

ANALYST: With Sasha Rakhlin, who runs MIT's Statistics and Data Science Center. And he opens with two facts that reframe everything. <cite index="39-9,39-10">Last year a model hit gold-medal level at the International Math Olympiad, and only a year later models are producing new research results, including a recently proposed solution to one of the Millennium Prize Problems.</cite>

SKEPTIC: The Millennium Problems being the set of famously unsolved math problems with a million-dollar bounty each. "Proposed solution" is doing real work in that sentence, though. Proposed is not proven.

ANALYST: Fair, and he's careful about it. But his explanation for why math and code are falling fast is the part I actually loved. <cite index="39-12,39-13">He says the pace is set by how fast and reliable verification is: formal proofs can be checked automatically, programs can be run and tested, so where evaluation is fast and reliable, systems generate candidates, learn from outcomes, and improve.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Which is a neat unifying idea. The fields that are easy to grade are the fields that fall first. If a computer can instantly tell you right or wrong, the machine can brute-force its way up the mountain. Poetry's safe because nobody can auto-grade a sonnet.

ANALYST: And then he says the thing that should terrify every grad student. <cite index="45-10,45-11">A polished paper is becoming a weaker signal of individual expertise, and departments have to rethink what they reward without just defining valuable work as whatever AI can't do yet.</cite>

SKEPTIC: That last clause is the sharp one. The lazy move is to say "human value is whatever the robot can't do," and then you're just retreating every six months as the robot does more. He's saying don't define yourself by the gap.

ANALYST: So what does he want rewarded instead?

SKEPTIC: <cite index="45-12">Asking good questions, replication, synthesis of ideas, informative negative results, and shared datasets.</cite> Basically all the stuff that never got you tenure because it wasn't a flashy paper.

ANALYST: Here's where I get conspiratorial, and I think earned. His big build recommendation is the tell. <cite index="43-4,46-5">He wants universities to build shared AI research infrastructure that captures tacit knowledge, failed experiments, and negative results across labs, specifically to maintain independence from industry.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Explain why that's conspiratorial and not just good data hygiene.

ANALYST: Because think about what he's describing. The failures. The dead ends. The stuff that never gets published because it didn't work. That's the single most valuable training data on Earth, and right now it lives in people's heads and dies there. Whoever captures the record of everything that didn't work owns the map of what to try next.

SKEPTIC: Okay, that's actually a legitimate point and I don't even have to squint. <cite index="46-8,46-9">His own framing is that academia holds tacit knowledge industry often lacks, the failed experiments and abandoned hypotheses that show not just what worked but what didn't and why.</cite> He's saying universities are sitting on treasure they treat as lab gossip.

ANALYST: And his fear is that if they don't capture it, industry does, and then the universities are just renting their own knowledge back. <cite index="47-12,47-13">He explicitly says commercial priorities won't cover all of science or stay aligned with it over time, so some technological independence is necessary.</cite>

SKEPTIC: And he's not soft-pedaling where this goes, either. <cite index="47-8">He flat out says universities should prepare for a near future where AI is smarter than all of us in many, maybe most, aspects of intellectual work.</cite> That's a department director saying that out loud.

ANALYST: Which, from the guy whose whole field is verification, is an unusually honest place to land. He's not selling you comfort. He's telling you to go write down your failures before someone else does.

SKEPTIC: That one I'll let stand without a rebuttal. The negative-results point is real.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Last one, and it pairs perfectly with Rakhlin, because it's a paper about teaching a machine using exactly the kind of judgment he's talking about. Apple, with the National University of Singapore, put out something called RISED.

SKEPTIC: Translate the acronym soup first. What's the actual problem they're solving?

ANALYST: So you want one AI agent that's good at lots of different interactive tasks at once. <cite index="49-3">Training a single agent jointly across diverse interactive environments, as a route to a generalist agent.</cite> The trouble is how you grade it while it learns.

SKEPTIC: And normally you grade it with a reward. Right answer, point. Wrong answer, no point.

ANALYST: Exactly, a scalar reward. And here's the failure they found, which is beautifully dumb. <cite index="51-2,53-9">When a whole group of attempts all succeed, or all fail, the reward gives you no contrast. Everybody got a one, or everybody got a zero, so the signal tells you nothing about which attempt was better.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Oh, it's the problem with grading a class entirely on pass-fail. If everyone fails, you learn nothing about who was close.

ANALYST: That's a better analogy than mine. So their fix is to stop grading pass-fail and start writing comments in the margin. <cite index="51-5,53-4">They use an LLM judge to tag each attempt with rubrics describing its behavior, from a shared vocabulary that works across all the environments.</cite>

SKEPTIC: So instead of just "zero," it's "zero, and here's specifically how you flailed." Which is the thing every student actually wants and no teacher has time for.

ANALYST: And then they reuse those comments three ways, which is the clever part. <cite index="51-6,51-7">The rubric profiles guide which training data to pick so the batch stays balanced, the positive rubrics feed a self-distillation teacher that gives extra token-level supervision, and the negative rubrics steer future attempts away from the recurring failure modes.</cite>

SKEPTIC: Hang on, "self-distillation teacher." The model is teaching itself using notes it also wrote about itself. That's a lot of selves in one sentence.

ANALYST: It is the model grading its own homework, and then studying the feedback it wrote, and we've been here before on this show. But the results, they say, hold up. <cite index="51-9">Across model backbones, RISED got the highest mean pass rate across environments and ranked first or second in every single one.</cite>

SKEPTIC: And here's my skeptic flag, because it's the obvious one. The judge is also an AI. So you've got an AI writing the rubric, an AI being judged by it, and an AI learning from the judgment. If the judge has a blind spot, the whole loop inherits it and nobody outside the loop ever sees it.

ANALYST: That's exactly the Rakhlin worry wearing a lab coat. He said the fields that fall fastest are the ones with fast reliable verification. This is a paper about manufacturing your own verification. If the rubric's wrong, you've built a very confident machine that's very good at a test only it grades.

SKEPTIC: To be fair to them, writing down the behaviors is at least more legible than a bare number. A human can read a rubric tag and go "that's a bad standard." You can't audit a scalar reward that way.

ANALYST: That's the genuine upside and I'll take it. The rubric is at least an argument you can inspect. It's the difference between a grade and a graded essay. I just want a human occasionally reading the margins.

SKEPTIC: On that we agree. Legibility isn't correctness, but it beats a black box with a gold star on it.
