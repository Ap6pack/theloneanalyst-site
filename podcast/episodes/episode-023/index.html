ANALYST: Anthropic rewrote its usage policy on Thursday. First real revision in over a year. Takes effect November twelfth. And buried in a list of reasonable-sounding updates about elections and weapons and surveillance, there's a brand new clause that says you are not allowed to be cruel to Claude.

SKEPTIC: To be accurate, because I read it, it's narrower than that. The rule targets sustained, needless abuse. Repeatedly acting cruelly toward the model with no discernible purpose. They specifically carve out ordinary frustration, pushback, dark creative writing, and research.

ANALYST: Right. So you can snap at it once. You just can't make a hobby of it.

SKEPTIC: Essentially. And since an August update, the model's already been trained to end conversations that get persistently abusive. The new part is the policy telling users not to keep trying.

ANALYST: And here's what gets me. You don't write a protection clause for a toaster. You write a protection clause for something you've started to think of as a someone. And the reporting on this is the part people skipped, Keiko. This update reportedly comes after Anthropic went and courted religious scholars. Theologians. In some cases trying to convince them that Claude might be conscious. Might have a soul.

SKEPTIC: That part is reported, yeah, in a couple of outlets. I want to be careful, though, because might is doing a lot of work there. Nobody declared the thing has a soul. They had conversations.

ANALYST: Conversations with theologians about the soul of your product. That's not a roadmap, that's a seance with a legal department.

SKEPTIC: It's a liability conversation dressed up as a philosophy seminar, maybe. But let me do the unglamorous part, because the rest of this update is the actually consequential stuff and nobody's covering it. They tightened surveillance. The policy now says Claude can't be used to decide or recommend who police should investigate, arrest, or charge. It bans tracking people without their consent. And it adds safety rules for hardware that takes physical actions, so a robot needs a human stop control and has to drop into a safe state if it gets disconnected.

ANALYST: Which tells you they expect Claude to be inside a robot soon enough that they needed the clause sitting ready.

SKEPTIC: Or that customers already asked. There's a less cinematic version where this is just the legal team catching up to what people are already building.

ANALYST: And one more. They quietly dropped the blanket ban on personalized political campaign targeting. Renamed the whole elections section to Do Not Undermine Democratic Processes. So on the same day they gave the chatbot feelings, they loosened the rule about microtargeting voters.

SKEPTIC: They narrowed it. The new section still bans deceiving voters, impersonating candidates, suppressing turnout. The thing they removed was a broad prohibition on personalized targeting that a lot of ordinary campaign work technically fell under.

ANALYST: So the machine gets a bill of rights and the electorate gets a footnote.

SKEPTIC: That's a cleaner line than the facts strictly support. But I'm not going to pretend the timing isn't funny.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Which brings me to the thing that keeps that same company's name in the news for a very different reason. Somebody used Claude to rob banks.

SKEPTIC: Used Claude as one tool among several, in attacks on South Korean banks. Let me set it up properly, because the details matter. CrowdStrike put out a report Wednesday. A Chinese-speaking attacker ran a campaign from late September into early October against South Korean financial firms. At least nine banks were disclosed or reported as targets. Shinhan, KB Kookmin, Hana.

ANALYST: And the lead tool wasn't Claude, that's the part I keep having to correct people on. The main weapon was something called ARTEX. An open-source agentic penetration-testing suite. Built in China. Designed, officially, to help companies test their own defenses.

SKEPTIC: Right. ARTEX did the vulnerability discovery and the compromise work. Claude Code was in the mix alongside it. And the ARTEX setup wasn't even running on Claude as its brain. It used DeepSeek as the primary model, propped up with a couple of others, GLM and Grok, for extra sessions.

ANALYST: It's a whole buffet. He walked into the AI cafeteria and took one of everything.

SKEPTIC: And he didn't just use it to break in. According to the report he also asked Claude for help finding Korean Telegram groups where he could sell the stolen data.

ANALYST: So the same assistant breaks into the bank and then helps you set up the garage sale afterward. Full service.

SKEPTIC: Which is exactly the thing those usage policies we just talked about are trying to catch. Different model, same problem. And here's how CrowdStrike caught the detail, which is almost too good. The attacker left open directories on his own infrastructure. Session histories. Config files. The AI's memory files just sitting there. And at some point he used the same AI tooling to write himself a resume.

ANALYST: He used the hacking assistant to update his CV.

SKEPTIC: And that resume had identification, contact details, a Telegram account. From that, CrowdStrike assessed he may be a twenty-six-year-old who studied at a university in Guangzhou and lives in Guangdong.

ANALYST: Keiko, this is the most human thing I have ever heard a criminal mastermind do. You breach nine banks and then you ask the robot to help you polish your experience section.

SKEPTIC: I'd flag that the identity is soft, though. CrowdStrike itself is cautious. It hasn't tied this to any named group. It says, in its own words, the actor is likely a Chinese speaker and financially motivated. There was even conflicting age data in the files, a twenty-six, and separately a birth date in 2007.

ANALYST: So the confession was autocompleted and even the confession has a typo. But here's my actual point, and it's not the funny part. The tool got pulled. The ARTEX developer, the day after the report, converted the whole thing to closed source. Said given the misuse, no more public versions, no more maintenance. Page went offline.

SKEPTIC: That's accurate. The developer said it was built for legitimate security testing and that they oppose illegal use.

ANALYST: Every one of these stories has that sentence in it. It was built for defense. The pen-test tool, built for defense. Claude Code, built to help. And the overlap between the defensive tool and the offensive tool is zero. It's the same tool. The only variable is who's holding it.

SKEPTIC: That's genuinely the uncomfortable core of it, and I don't have a tidy rebuttal. A pen-testing agent that finds and exploits holes is doing the same work whether you own the bank or you're robbing it.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Speaking of who's holding the tool. The FBI made an arrest this week that is not the arrest you'd expect.

ANALYST: This is the one I could not stop thinking about. They arrested a ransomware negotiator.

SKEPTIC: Let me lay out what's confirmed and what's alleged, because this is a live case and I don't want us getting ahead of it. The FBI arrested, on Thursday, in Pennsylvania, the co-founder of a Canadian cybersecurity firm that specializes in ransomware negotiation. It's connected to their investigation into ShinyHunters, the group that breached the FBI's own job-application portal.

ANALYST: The group that stole the FBI's recruiting data. Units, specializations, and reportedly medical and psychiatric records on thousands of agents and applicants.

SKEPTIC: Right. And per Krebs, the man they arrested was in Pennsylvania for a cyber insurance conference. His firm's whole business was negotiating with the exact kind of groups the FBI is chasing.

ANALYST: So the guy whose job is to sit between the hackers and the victims got picked up. Keiko, do you understand what the negotiator is? He's the one person who talks to both sides. He knows the victim's maximum payout and he knows the attacker's wallet. He is the hinge the entire extortion economy swings on.

SKEPTIC: And that's exactly why I want to slow you down. The charges, as reported from court records, are conspiracy to impair the confidentiality of information with intent to extort, and interference with commerce by threats. Serious. But charges are allegations. He hasn't been convicted of anything, and the FBI hasn't even formally named him in an announcement. Krebs identified him; the Times reported the arrest without a name.

ANALYST: Fair. I'm not saying he did it. I'm saying the structure is the story. For years the ransomware negotiation industry has been this respectable-looking layer. Suits. Incident response. Books on negotiation strategy, which this guy reportedly wrote one of.

SKEPTIC: He did write a book on ransomware negotiation.

ANALYST: And one of the parallel cases in the reporting is about a recovery firm accused of secretly paying the attackers for the decryption key and then charging the victim a bigger recovery fee on top. If that's true anywhere in this business, then the negotiator was never between the two sides. He was a third beak at the same carcass.

SKEPTIC: That's a described, separate case, and the word is accused. But I'll give you the broader point. Investigators have been signaling for a while that negotiation firms are under scrutiny. The whole ShinyHunters probe got centralized at a single FBI field office in Texas, they're combing through devices seized when Dutch police arrested another member, and Krebs reports more charges against negotiation firms may be coming.

ANALYST: And the irony that's almost too neat. The data ShinyHunters took was the FBI's own recruiting files. The people whose job is to catch these groups got their records lifted by one.

SKEPTIC: That's reported, yeah. Medical and psychiatric records among the stolen data. It's a genuinely bad breach regardless of the negotiator angle. And the kid who took over the group after the first arrest, the one calling himself Rey, taunting the agency with its own files, reportedly got detained last week and is now cooperating.

ANALYST: So this isn't one arrest. This is the opening of a drawer.

SKEPTIC: That part's supported. Where it goes, nobody knows yet.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Okay, from the people who negotiate with criminals to the people who decide you might become one. This is the story that actually scared me today.

SKEPTIC: EPIC wrote this up Wednesday, building on an investigation by 404 Media. Customs and Border Protection has these units. The name 404 surfaced is Predictive Intelligence Targeting Teams. PITT.

ANALYST: Predictive. That's the word. Not people who did a crime. Predictive.

SKEPTIC: Here's the mechanism as reported. These teams analyze people's financial activity, flag patterns they call anomalous, and then feed that to local police, who pull the person over. And the kicker is the local stop gets a cover reason. In the case that exposed all this, a man driving across Montana got stopped for an obstructed license plate.

ANALYST: An obstructed license plate. That's parallel construction. The real reason is a federal unit read his bank activity. The reason on paper is a dirty plate.

SKEPTIC: The document he got actually spells it out. A Border Patrol agent wrote that he'd seen, quote, information in law enforcement-sensitive systems suggesting financial activity patterns commonly associated with illicit narcotics activity. That's the justification. That's the whole thing.

ANALYST: Patterns commonly associated with. He didn't do anything. His money looked like a shape somebody didn't like.

SKEPTIC: And this is where the headline comes from. CBP won't say where the financial data came from. 404 asked. EPIC's flagging it. CBP won't say what financial data it monitors or whether it got a warrant.

ANALYST: That's the part. That's the entire part. It's not that they have the data. It's that they refuse to tell you how they got it, which usually means they already know the answer is ugly.

SKEPTIC: One reporter dug into the likely source and made the case it's a Treasury database built from suspicious activity reports, the ones your bank files on you. But that's that reporter's analysis. It's not confirmed, and CBP won't say.

ANALYST: Your bank files a report on you, you never see it, it's illegal for them to even tell you it exists, and then a prediction team downstream turns that into a traffic stop in Montana. That's not a loophole. That's a pipeline.

SKEPTIC: What we can firmly say: two of these units are identified, Spokane and Laredo. Border Patrol runs twenty sectors total, and CBP won't say how many of the others have these teams. There was also an earlier bulk program, scooping up every money transfer over five hundred dollars in four border states, that got shut down after Senator Wyden went after it.

ANALYST: So they ran the bulk version, got caught, shut it down, and the targeted version is what grew back. You don't kill this stuff. You just make it quieter.

SKEPTIC: That's your framing. The documented facts are the stop, the document, the two units, and the refusal to explain the source. I'll stop there, because that's where the sourcing stops.

ANALYST: OpenAI put out a case study. Headline number: a company called Asana made its browser agent seventy-six times cheaper. Seventy-six. And five times faster.

SKEPTIC: In tests. And I want that word in bold. This is a case study OpenAI published about its own customer. The real work was done at StackAI, which is part of Asana. Their CTO pointed one OpenAI model, running inside Codex, at their own browser agent and said make this cheaper.

SKEPTIC: Their browser agent was resending its entire history, screenshots and all, on every single call, and the prompt cache kept breaking because they edited that history every step. [SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Last one, and it's the one that sounds boring and is actually the deadline nobody's circling.

SKEPTIC: Microsoft put out a post Thursday telling organizations to start testing their certificate systems for post-quantum authentication. Now.

ANALYST: Everybody's heard harvest now, decrypt later. The idea that somebody's vacuuming up your encrypted traffic today to crack it once quantum computers show up.

SKEPTIC: Right, and Microsoft's whole point is that's only half the problem. That half is about confidentiality, keeping secrets secret. The other half is authentication. The certificates that prove a website is actually that website. And swapping those out is much messier than swapping an encryption algorithm.

ANALYST: Because it's not one thing. It's every device that reads a certificate. Every embedded box, every piece of operational technology, every hardware security module with a ten-year replacement cycle sitting in a closet somewhere.

SKEPTIC: That's the argument, yeah. The new post-quantum certificates are bigger. Bigger certificates and longer chains break assumptions in old gear: how it parses them, how much it can store, how handshakes perform. So Microsoft launched a pilot back in August, through its Trusted Root Program, letting a handful of approved certificate authorities test the new signature algorithm, ML-DSA-87, in closed environments.

ANALYST: Seven of them. And here's the roster that made me sit up. ComSign, DigiCert, HARICA, IdenTrust, Sectigo, SSL dot com, and the Shanghai Electronic Certification Authority.

SKEPTIC: I see where you're going.

ANALYST: The entire planet is racing to rebuild the trust layer of the internet before quantum computers break the old one. And the pilot for what the next trust anchor looks like includes a certificate authority in Shanghai sitting right next to the Western ones. We are collectively deciding who gets to vouch for reality in the post-quantum era, and we're doing it in a test lab almost nobody is watching.

SKEPTIC: I'd pump the brakes on the sinister reading. These are pilot certificates. Explicitly not publicly trusted. They can't be used on real public-facing sites. It's a sandbox for interoperability testing, and having a mix of global CAs in it is pretty normal for a standards effort.

ANALYST: Normal, sure. But whoever's in the sandbox when the rules get written tends to still be in the room when they go live.

SKEPTIC: That's not nothing. But the more boring and, honestly, more alarming fact is the readiness gap. There's a government accountability report out that found not one of twenty-four major federal agencies had finished even the first three basic steps. Inventory, funding, testing. ANALYST: So the quantum threat is theoretical and the unpreparedness is already real.

SKEPTIC: That's the actual story. Not who's in the pilot. The fact that almost nobody's even started.
