[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Start with the big one. The EFF just laid out what they're calling federal data consolidation, and the frame is that over the last two years the government quietly merged the databases it swore it would never merge.

SKEPTIC: Let me put the sourcing on it, because "quietly" is carrying weight. EFF describes it arriving in three rolling waves. <cite index="1-3">It started with DOGE, moved to agency-to-agency data sharing with ICE, and then attempted data-driven purges of state voter rolls.</cite>

ANALYST: Three waves. That's not an accident. That's a pipeline with stages.

SKEPTIC: Or three separate things that happened in sequence. Walk me through wave one.

ANALYST: Wave one is DOGE, and the origin reads like the opening of a heist. <cite index="1-6">Starting January twentieth, 2025, Trump created DOGE by essentially renaming an existing agency that was originally meant to modernize government technology.</cite>

SKEPTIC: And instead of modernizing anything, <cite index="1-7">it quickly obtained high-level access to sensitive databases across the government that store millions of people's personal records, including at the Office of Personnel Management, the Social Security Administration, and the Treasury.</cite>

ANALYST: And who's staffing this? Career data stewards who've spent decades thinking about what Social Security records can do in the wrong hands?

SKEPTIC: No. <cite index="1-8">It was staffed with people who had little government or cybersecurity knowledge and who often had conflicts of interest.</cite> And reporting beyond EFF backs the shape of it. <cite index="3-4,3-5">DOGE reportedly worked to unify data into a single centralized database, helping integrate once-segregated government datasets into what's been described as a master database at DHS.</cite>

ANALYST: A master database. They said the quiet part into a project spec. And there's one detail inside this I genuinely cannot stop looking at. Somebody at Social Security signed a side deal.

SKEPTIC: Yeah. According to EFF's account, <cite index="6-7">an individual working with DOGE at the Social Security Administration signed an outside agreement with an advocacy group intended to use SSA data to find evidence of alleged voter fraud and overturn election results in certain states.</cite>

ANALYST: Read that back slowly. Public benefits data, repurposed to overturn elections. That's not a glitch. That's the whole machine compressed into one sentence.

SKEPTIC: It's one individual and one agreement, and I'm not going to inflate it into "the whole machine." But it is precisely the kind of thing the Privacy Act was written to stop.

ANALYST: The seventies.

SKEPTIC: 1974. <cite index="1-4,1-5">Congress flagged abuses like enemies lists and snooping on activists and federal employees after the McCarthy and Watergate eras, and the growing use of computers to magnify those harms led to protections like the Privacy Act of 1974.</cite>

ANALYST: Wave two is the one that makes wave one dangerous. The data's already pooled, and now ICE wants to use it.

SKEPTIC: Right. <cite index="3-7">While some of these agreements have been challenged in court, ICE is seeking to repurpose this information to locate individuals for deportation, affecting immigrants and U.S. citizens who provided the data to pay taxes or receive services.</cite>

ANALYST: You paid your taxes. You did the responsible thing. And the receipt becomes an address for enforcement.

SKEPTIC: That's the chilling-effect argument, and it's EFF's central one. When people believe their benefits data can be turned against them, they stop participating. They don't file, they don't enroll, they don't register.

ANALYST: Which is the point. A database you're afraid of controls you without ever having to be used.

SKEPTIC: And wave three is the voter rolls, through the SAVE system. The numbers EFF cites are what should make people on every side uneasy. <cite index="6-9">More than sixty million registered voters have been processed through SAVE, resulting in twenty-one thousand flags for potential noncitizen status and subsequent voter roll purges.</cite>

ANALYST: Sixty million checked to find twenty-one thousand. That's a net the size of the ocean to catch a bucket of fish.

SKEPTIC: And twenty-one thousand flags doesn't mean twenty-one thousand actual noncitizens. A flag is a question, not a conviction. That's the core problem with database matching, it manufactures suspicion at scale.

ANALYST: Every one of these databases was collected with a promise. Give us your data to get benefits, pay taxes, register to vote, and we keep it in its lane. Three waves is the lane disappearing.

SKEPTIC: And the government's counterargument is efficiency. One unified view of the citizen.

ANALYST: One view of the citizen is also the dictionary definition of a surveillance state. Those are the same sentence with different lighting.

SKEPTIC: That one I'll give you.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Next, the security roundup. And I want the dark web story first, because that one actually ended.

SKEPTIC: Empire Market. <cite index="12-2">Raheim Hamilton, thirty, of Virginia, was sentenced to forty years in prison and fined five million dollars after pleading guilty to a drug conspiracy charge over Empire Market, the dark web marketplace he co-created and ran with Thomas Pavey from 2018 to 2020.</cite>

ANALYST: Forty years. That's not a slap on the wrist, that's the rest of a life handed over.

SKEPTIC: The scale explains the number. <cite index="14-1">The marketplace handled more than four hundred thirty million dollars in illegal transactions.</cite> And <cite index="15-6,15-7">more than four million transactions, with drug sales alone around three hundred seventy-five million, dealers selling fentanyl, cocaine, meth, and heroin alongside hacking tools and stolen information.</cite>

ANALYST: Two guys, two years, and then it just vanished into air.

SKEPTIC: Empire went dark in 2020. And the backstory is almost a corporate résumé. <cite index="15-9">Before launching their own operation, Hamilton and Pavey worked together selling counterfeit U.S. currency on AlphaBay, the marketplace authorities shut down in 2017.</cite>

ANALYST: So AlphaBay goes down, and the vacancy gets filled by its own vendors. You don't kill the market. You just promote the middle managers.

SKEPTIC: And it's not fully closed out. <cite index="18-5">His co-founder is set to be sentenced later this month.</cite>

ANALYST: Here's the detail that made me laugh and then stop laughing. Prosecutors say concealment was baked into the review system.

SKEPTIC: Yeah, the DOJ said <cite index="14-12">Hamilton and Pavey designed the platform to help users avoid law enforcement and launder money from illegal transactions.</cite> Which made <cite index="14-13">concealment part of the marketplace's review system, rather than simply a private concern for individuals.</cite>

ANALYST: They gamified not getting caught. It's a Yelp where the five-star criterion is "the cops never found out."

SKEPTIC: Same roundup had two items that matter more to ordinary people than a dark web sentencing. One's about GPUs.

ANALYST: Oh, I love this one. The machines that run the entire AI economy were leaving the front lights on.

SKEPTIC: Measured, please. <cite index="12-3,12-4">Researchers disclosed a high-severity flaw in Nvidia's DCGM Exporter GPU monitoring tool, where unauthenticated attackers can flood its profiling endpoints to exhaust resources and crash the service, potentially slowing AI workloads running on the same host.</cite>

ANALYST: And how many were just sitting open to the internet?

SKEPTIC: <cite index="12-5">In scans between March and May 2026, researchers found roughly twenty-one hundred hosts exposing the exporter without authentication, leaking telemetry from more than twelve thousand GPUs.</cite> <cite index="12-6">Nvidia has fixed it, and users are advised to update to version 4.8.2 or later.</cite>

ANALYST: Twelve thousand GPUs broadcasting their own vital signs. These are the most contested objects on the planet right now, and a couple thousand of them had no door on the room.

SKEPTIC: It's the monitoring tool, not the GPUs themselves, and the leak is telemetry, not your model weights. But yeah, it's sloppy at a scale that should embarrass people.

ANALYST: Telemetry is a map, though. You tell me which machines are busy and how hot they're running, and I know exactly where the valuable work is happening without touching a single thing.

SKEPTIC: That's actually a reasonable read.

ANALYST: And the third one is the worm.

SKEPTIC: The Tensorlake npm package. <cite index="12-7">Version 0.5.144 of tensorlake, the npm SDK for its AI agent sandboxes, was compromised to run a credential-stealing worm during installation, in what Socket describes as a ChainDrop or Shai-Hulud supply chain attack.</cite> <cite index="12-8">It harvests npm, GitHub, AWS, Kubernetes, and Vault credentials, plus AI coding tool configurations.</cite>

ANALYST: So the thing that steals your keys is riding inside the toolkit for building AI agents. The sandbox had a hole in the floor.

SKEPTIC: And it fires on install, which is the worst possible moment, because that's when people are least watching what a package does.

ANALYST: Three stories, one roundup, and the connective tissue is: the fence is always lower than the thing it's guarding.

SKEPTIC: Sometimes there's no fence at all. That's not a conspiracy. That's just defaults nobody changed.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Which rolls straight into the Talos roundup, because that's a whole pile of fences that only just got built.

SKEPTIC: Cisco Talos disclosed a batch. <cite index="45-5">The roundup covers one Adobe Photoshop issue, one macOS CoreWLAN issue, two Foxit Reader flaws, and four Microsoft Windows vulnerabilities.</cite> All already patched by the vendors.

ANALYST: Start with Foxit, because PDFs are where I keep most of my paranoia.

SKEPTIC: Reasonable place to keep it. <cite index="38-6,38-7">One is a code execution vulnerability in the JavaScript checkbox widget functionality of Foxit Reader, where a specially crafted malformed file can lead to remote code execution.</cite> The other's a use-after-free in how it handles an array object.

ANALYST: A checkbox. In a PDF. Can run code on my machine.

SKEPTIC: Through JavaScript, yes. <cite index="42-8,42-9">Foxit supports JavaScript for interactive documents and dynamic forms, and that support is an additional attack surface. It uses the V8 engine.</cite>

ANALYST: People think a PDF is a piece of paper. It is not a piece of paper. It is a program wearing a piece of paper as a costume.

SKEPTIC: That's annoyingly accurate. A modern PDF can run scripts, and that's the entire reason this class of bug keeps existing.

ANALYST: And the Photoshop one?

SKEPTIC: <cite index="38-2,38-3">That's a privilege escalation vulnerability in the installation functionality of Photoshop, where an attacker can replace files with a specially crafted malformed file to trigger it and escalate privileges.</cite>

ANALYST: The installer. The one moment you hand software the keys to the whole house, trusting it to only redecorate one room.

SKEPTIC: And there are the Windows ones, including <cite index="38-10,38-11">an out-of-bounds pointer offset flaw in the Windows NETIO.sys driver, where a specially crafted I/O request packet can cause disclosure of sensitive information.</cite>

ANALYST: Now, there's genuinely good news buried in all of this, which I will say out loud and then immediately distrust: these are all patched.

SKEPTIC: They are. <cite index="38-1">Talos's Vulnerability Discovery and Research team disclosed them across Adobe, Apple, Foxit, and Microsoft.</cite> The whole point of coordinated disclosure is that the fix ships before the gory details do.

ANALYST: So why do I distrust it?

SKEPTIC: You tell me. You're the one who distrusts it.

ANALYST: Because "patched" means "patched for the people who update." And the entire previous story was twenty-one hundred machines that never updated. The patch exists in a lab. Whether it exists on your actual computer is a completely different question.

SKEPTIC: That part's just true. A patch nobody installs is a rumor with a version number.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Okay, lighter one. Or I thought it was lighter, until I read it twice. Somebody at Hugging Face needed a model that didn't exist, so they had a robot build it overnight.

SKEPTIC: Let me describe it straight first. A Hugging Face team member wanted a smaller version of the prompt rewriter that ships with an image model. <cite index="21-4,21-5,21-6">The official one is a 9B model that needs about twenty gigs of memory and thinks for thousands of tokens, and on the Hub they only found compressed copies of that same model, so they described what they wanted to an agent called ML-Intern, and the next day they had a 0.8B version that runs on a CPU.</cite>

ANALYST: The next day. For how much?

SKEPTIC: <cite index="21-7,21-8">It returns valid output 99.7 percent of the time, uses about a quarter of the teacher's tokens, and the whole project, including having the 9B model label almost eighty-eight hundred example requests, came to sixteen dollars.</cite>

ANALYST: Sixteen dollars. To mint a working model overnight. And then it didn't stop there?

SKEPTIC: No. <cite index="21-9">Over the next few days they made five more models the same way.</cite> <cite index="24-5">All six projects together cost about a hundred and three dollars in compute.</cite>

ANALYST: So what does the robot actually do, step by step? Because this is where it stops being cute.

SKEPTIC: It's an agent living inside HuggingChat. <cite index="23-5">From a detailed prompt, it plans the work, asks for a budget before it spends anything, runs a small test before the real job, trains, evaluates, and publishes the resulting model to the Hub, all on Hugging Face infrastructure.</cite>

ANALYST: It asks for a budget. It runs a smoke test. It resubmits its own failed jobs. Keiko, that's not a tool. That's an employee.

SKEPTIC: It's an employee that costs a few dollars a project and doesn't need a desk or a dental plan. And one of the results is genuinely impressive. <cite index="22-4">A citrus disease vision model went from 14.9 percent accuracy up to 52.8 percent, for a dollar ninety.</cite>

ANALYST: A dollar ninety to more than triple the accuracy of a model that diagnoses sick plants. You realize what that means. The barrier to making a specialized model just fell to the price of a coffee.

SKEPTIC: Which is mostly good. More people can build the exact niche thing they need, instead of waiting for a giant lab to get around to it.

ANALYST: And the flip side, which lives in the same loop. That half-a-day, hundred-dollar pipeline builds the helpful plant doctor and the thing you'd really rather nobody built. The loop doesn't know the difference.

SKEPTIC: The loop doesn't, no. The person writing the prompt does. That's not nothing.

ANALYST: It's not nothing. It's just a lot thinner than it used to be. The guardrail is now one human and their conscience, at a dollar ninety a pop.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Which is the perfect setup for the Google one, because it asks the question directly: if the AI does better work, does that make a better worker?

SKEPTIC: And the answer is genuinely interesting, so let me get it right. This is a three-month randomized controlled trial with practicing patent attorneys. <cite index="30-8">They randomized access to a then-unreleased Google Labs AI patent writing tool, now part of Gemini Notebook, among a hundred and thirty-three lawyers at eleven IP law firms that do regular, non-exclusive business with Google.</cite>

ANALYST: Note that phrase, "firms that do business with Google." Hold onto that. What did they find?

SKEPTIC: <cite index="30-3">Routinely using AI lifts work quality across the board, but the effect on on-the-job learning depends on seniority. Senior lawyers who used it for ninety days showed stronger judgment than those who didn't. Junior lawyers showed no average skill gain, their scores splitting into more strong scores and more low scores.</cite>

ANALYST: Stop. That splitting part. That is the entire story.

SKEPTIC: Say why.

ANALYST: The seniors already have judgment, so the AI is a lever under it. The juniors are still building judgment, and the tool either supercharges them or quietly rots them, and on average it looks like nothing happened because the two groups cancel each other out.

SKEPTIC: That's actually close to how they frame it. <cite index="30-4,30-5">Judgment is what separates senior professionals from juniors, and building it usually takes thousands of hours of tedious but formative work, often under guided supervision.</cite> The worry is that AI eats exactly that tedious, formative work.

ANALYST: The ladder. They're pulling up the ladder. Not on purpose, but the rungs were the boring tasks, and the robot just took all the boring tasks.

SKEPTIC: And yet the same body of research cuts the other way too. <cite index="30-7">Studies in radiology, business problem-solving, job-seeker writing, and legal education show that when AI is thoughtfully built into training, less-experienced workers can improve their independent performance.</cite> So it's not destiny. It's design.

ANALYST: "Thoughtfully built into training." Who's doing the thoughtful building? Because the default, the just-hand-everyone-the-tool default, is the thing that produced the split.

SKEPTIC: That's fair. The default outcome in this study wasn't a clean, uniform boost for juniors. It was a divide.

ANALYST: Now back to the thing I told you to hold. The firms do business with Google. The tool was a Google tool. And the conclusion is "AI helps, if it's designed well," where the designer is...

SKEPTIC: I knew you'd land there. It's a real limitation, and they disclose the relationship, which is the correct thing to do. It doesn't automatically poison the finding. But you read a company's study of its own product with your eyes open.

ANALYST: Eyes open. That's all I'm ever asking for.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Last one, and it's the one that genuinely unsettled me, because it's the government quietly admitting it's blind.

SKEPTIC: This is a Databricks post, so frame it as what it is: a vendor making a pitch. But the underlying problem they describe is real and honestly staggering. <cite index="48-8,48-9">They say the Department of War cannot see its own supply chain, and that directives since 2020, culminating in an August 2026 cost and pricing transparency memo, now require actual cost data and supply chain mapping across a base of more than two hundred thousand suppliers.</cite>

ANALYST: Department of War. That's the term they're using in this.

SKEPTIC: That's the term in the post, yes. And the status quo they describe is almost quaint. <cite index="48-12,48-13">Cost and pricing evidence moves through manual data calls and fragmented spreadsheets, and lower-tier supplier origins are largely unknown.</cite>

ANALYST: Spreadsheets. The largest procurement operation on earth is tracking two hundred thousand suppliers with emails and spreadsheets.

SKEPTIC: And the pitch is a federated exchange. <cite index="49-3">Contractors would govern their own data in place and publish versioned, quality-checked evidence to named government recipients using Databricks' open sharing protocol, instead of manual data calls or a centralized ERP integration.</cite>

ANALYST: "Govern their own data in place." Translate that for me.

SKEPTIC: Nobody hands over their raw systems. <cite index="48-4">Every tier of the supply chain publishes validated evidence from existing records without exposing the source systems.</cite> The selling point is <cite index="48-5">no vendor lock-in, no costly platform overhaul, built on open protocols.</cite>

ANALYST: Here's my paranoid read, and I'll own that it's paranoid. You build the pipe that connects every defense supplier's data to "named government recipients," and you have just built the single most valuable map in the country. Who makes what, what it costs, when it's late, where the one bolt comes from.

SKEPTIC: Which is also the exact map the Pentagon says it needs so it doesn't get blindsided by a broken supply chain. The same visibility that worries you is the thing that stops a weapons program from discovering too late that a critical part comes from one single factory.

ANALYST: Both things are true at once.

SKEPTIC: They usually are. The real question here isn't whether the map is powerful. It's who gets to read it and under what rules, and a vendor blog is never going to answer that part.

ANALYST: The pilot's small, at least.

SKEPTIC: It is. <cite index="49-4">The proposed pilot defines two data contracts, cost-and-pricing and supplier-and-production status, across a small number of primes on a single program.</cite> Start small, prove it, scale it.

ANALYST: "Then it's everywhere" is the four scariest words in all of enterprise software.

SKEPTIC: On that, we agree completely.
