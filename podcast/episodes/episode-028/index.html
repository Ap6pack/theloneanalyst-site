SKEPTIC: Which isn't new. Personal Intelligence, launched in early 2026, lets users pipe data from Gmail, Drive, and Photos into Google's AI products, Gemini, AI Overviews, and AI Mode search. The pitch is convenience. You ask it to plan a trip, it reads your hotel confirmation and actually knows what you're talking about.

You can disconnect the apps or turn off personalized recommendations.

SKEPTIC: Okay, but if you think the ads reading you is bad, there's a story this week about ads doing something a lot worse than reading you.

ANALYST: This is the one that actually kept me up. Lay out the facts, because I don't want anyone thinking I made this up in the dark.

SKEPTIC: Citizen Lab, at the University of Toronto, with the ad-tech firm Confiant. They published a report about a technique they're calling AdFection. Citizen Lab says it has documented the first publicly confirmed case of zero-click mercenary spyware being delivered through targeted online ads.

ANALYST: Zero-click. I want to sit on those two words. No tap. No link. No shady download.

SKEPTIC: Correct. The ad carried a small script that, the moment it loaded, phoned a Discover-Tech server with the device's ad ID and IP address. If the server decided you were a target, it sent back exploit code the browser ran on its own. You load an ordinary website, the ad renders, and that's the whole attack. In some cases the ad even loaded out of sight, in the background.

ANALYST: And it wasn't theoretical. Who actually got hit, and on what?

SKEPTIC: Here's where I have to be precise, because the easy version gets it wrong. Citizen Lab confirmed that at least 45 devices belonging to staff at Al Jazeera were targeted across 2024 and 2025. The ones they could forensically pin down were Windows computers. Al Jazeera recovered a Windows DLL from one compromised machine, and that implant is what let the lab tie it to Candiru, an Israeli spyware vendor, with high confidence. And the bridge into the ad world was an Israeli firm called Discover-Tech.

ANALYST: So not phones, as I'd assumed. Windows machines in a newsroom.

SKEPTIC: Right, that's the confirmed part. Now, separate from that, the ad network itself had enormous reach, including mobile. Over a four-month stretch the researchers found Discover-Tech ads served across nearly 200,000 websites and Android and iOS apps in dozens of countries. But reach is not infection. The report is careful: at least 45 Al Jazeera devices targeted, and they couldn't even prove all 45 were successfully compromised.

ANALYST: Good, keep me honest on that, because the scary number and the confirmed number are different. Still, the pattern is the thing. Discover-Tech wasn't lurking in some dark corner. It was on the guest list.

SKEPTIC: That's the part that should make the industry wince. The company had been listed as a certified or approved vendor by Google and IAB Europe since 2020, so its ads ran through major exchanges including Google, Yandex and Comcast's Beeswax. Certified. Approved. For about six years.

ANALYST: So the surveillance pipeline came with a badge that said trusted partner. This is the thing I've been saying on this show for weeks, and I hate being right about it. The real-time bidding system broadcasts enough about who and where you are that a buyer can single out one machine in a newsroom and fire at it. Surveillance capitalism and actual surveillance were always the same pipe. This is the receipt.

SKEPTIC: And now the policy fight. Two senators, Ron Wyden and Cory Booker, Democrats, both say the report proves the industry has to clean house. Back in 2023 they introduced a bill to stop ad networks from using personal data to target, and the legislation never advanced past introduction. Now they're pointing at AdFection and saying, see.

ANALYST: And the industry's response is the part that tells you everything. What did the ad groups say?

SKEPTIC: The headline is almost funny. The senators say targeted ads are a security risk, and the ad groups say the critics are off base. IAB Europe and Google defend their vetting practices. Google did say it cut Discover-Tech off for systematically violating its policies, blocked it from its ad exchanges, and that it collaborated with the investigation. IAB Europe suspended it after an audit, and Beeswax dropped it too.

ANALYST: They defend the vetting process that approved the spyware vendor for six years. That's not a defense, that's a confession wearing a tie.

SKEPTIC: I'll push back a little. They did remove it, and Google participated in the investigation. That's not nothing. And Citizen Lab's own pointed line was that the links to the spyware world were sitting right there in corporate records. So the failure reads less like a secret plot and more like nobody bothering to look.

ANALYST: Keiko, nobody bothering to look is the most dangerous system in the world. A deliberate conspiracy has a brain you can fight. Institutional indifference just runs forever and calls itself a marketplace.

SKEPTIC: And the uncomfortable kicker is the official advice here isn't new either. The report notes U.S. agencies warned years ago that adversaries may leverage malicious ads to deploy malware, and recommended that government agencies use ad-blocking software.

ANALYST: The government told its own people to install ad blockers for national security, and the rest of us got told ad blockers are stealing from content creators. Two messages, two audiences, same spyware.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Speaking of the government and data it shouldn't have been near, there was an actual court ruling this week, and it's a big one.

SKEPTIC: This is the OPM and DOGE case. And this one I'll let you be loud about, because the judge basically was. Judge Denise Cote in the Southern District of New York found that OPM violated the Privacy Act by handing DOGE access to the sensitive data of tens of millions of federal employees.

ANALYST: Tens of millions. Walk people back to the beginning, because this started almost two years ago.

SKEPTIC: February 2025. The American Federation of Government Employees led a group of plaintiffs in suing OPM for permitting DOGE to access records on federal employees, including personally identifiable information, in the early part of the Trump administration. Backed by EFF and a few others. There was a preliminary injunction last June, and now, this week, the summary judgment.

ANALYST: And the ruling is specific. It's not a vibe, it's two provisions of the Privacy Act.

SKEPTIC: Right. The court found that OPM violated two provisions of the Privacy Act, one restricting disclosure of personal records between agencies and another requiring agencies to protect the security and confidentiality of records. So both the handing-over and the failure to safeguard.

ANALYST: Here's the detail that I cannot get over, and you tell me if I've got it right. The government barely even argued they were allowed to do it.

SKEPTIC: You've got it right, and it's almost the whole story. The judge wrote that at this point, there is no meaningful dispute concerning the underlying conduct. The defendants barely defend its legality, relying instead on what has happened since to argue that the case is moot and that prospective relief is unnecessary.

ANALYST: That's a legal strategy of we stopped, so who cares. Imagine getting caught with the keys to every federal worker's file and your defense is I already put them back.

SKEPTIC: And Cote specifically rejected the excuse they did offer. She said the record provides no support for a finding that it was the pursuit of a modernization agenda that prompted OPM to grant sweeping and expedited access to a score of DOGE Agents to its systems of records containing PII. Basically, modernization didn't need the Social Security numbers.

ANALYST: A score of DOGE agents. Twenty-ish people, described in the lawsuit as unvetted and untrained, got broad access to the personnel records of the entire federal workforce. And the official framing was efficiency.

SKEPTIC: That's the plaintiffs' characterization, unvetted and untrained, and the judge's own earlier language was blunt too. Last June she called it a gross departure from OPM's obligations under the Privacy Act and its longstanding cybersecurity practices.

ANALYST: So here's my worry, and it's not the paranoid version, it's the boring version, which is scarier. DOGE is disbanded. Musk is gone. The agents left. And the data? Nobody can fully account for the copies.

SKEPTIC: That's real and still unresolved. Plaintiffs are still seeking to delete or expunge any copies of sensitive OPM data that DOGE agents still possess. And Cote noted the scope of relief gets decided in a separate ruling.

ANALYST: So the ruling says it was illegal, but the question of where the data actually went is a sequel. The breach is confirmed. The cleanup is to be announced.

SKEPTIC: And the judge even flagged the recurrence risk. She found that DOGE being shut down only narrows, doesn't eliminate, the chance this happens again, because the failure was individuals ignoring safeguards that already existed.

ANALYST: Which is the real lesson. The locks were on the doors. The rules were written. None of it mattered, because the whole thing ran on the honor system and the honor was optional.

SKEPTIC: I'll land here with you, mostly. It's a genuine win for the privacy side and a rare one. I just want to keep the caveat attached: a court saying it was illegal and the data actually being gone are two different finish lines, and only one of them got crossed this week.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Switching worlds. Anthropic did something this week that on the surface looks generous, and I want to figure out if the generosity is the story or the cover.

SKEPTIC: This is the Agent Skills donation. And before you spin it, the facts are pretty clean. Anthropic donated Agent Skills, the open standard that lets an AI agent load a folder of instructions for a specific task, to the Agentic AI Foundation on October 22, 2026. The foundation sits under the Linux Foundation; Anthropic co-founded it with Block and OpenAI, and it already holds the Model Context Protocol.

ANALYST: Explain what a skill actually is, because the normal person hears Agent Skills and pictures nothing.

SKEPTIC: It's deliberately simple. A skill is a folder with a SKILL.md file that holds a name, a description and instructions, and can bundle scripts, references and assets. You hand an AI agent that folder, and now it knows how to do a specific job your way. Think a recipe card the model can pick up when the task matches.

ANALYST: And the adoption numbers are not small. This went from a Claude feature to an industry thing fast.

SKEPTIC: Very fast. More than 70 agent platforms, including ChatGPT, Cursor, GitHub Copilot and VS Code, have integrated Agent Skills since the standard was published in December 2025. One million skills have been published and nearly 280 million installed. Those are Anthropic's own numbers, worth noting, not independently audited.

ANALYST: So here's the pattern that lights me up. This is the second time they've run this exact play. They did it with the Model Context Protocol, now they're doing it with Skills. Donate the standard to a neutral foundation you helped create. Give away the name, the logo, even the domain.

SKEPTIC: They did literally give those away. Anthropic announced it was donating the Agent Skills standard, the name, the logo, the GitHub organization, and the agentskills.io domain to the Agentic AI Foundation. Which does sound like a lot to hand over.

ANALYST: It is a lot. And that's what makes it brilliant rather than charitable. If you own the standard, you're a vendor people have to trust. If a neutral foundation owns the standard, everyone adopts it freely, and you're just the company that happened to invent how all the agents talk. You don't own the railroad. You own the fact that it's your gauge of track.

SKEPTIC: That's basically what the commentary said out loud, that it's the MCP playbook again, not philanthropy. I don't even think that's a dark read. It's a strategy, and it can be a smart strategy and a genuinely good thing for interoperability at the same time. Those aren't mutually exclusive.

ANALYST: Agreed, and I'll say the quiet part. I actually like vendor-neutral standards. What I'm watching is the security hole underneath all this. A skill isn't just text. It can bundle scripts. Executable code.

SKEPTIC: And that's the part the coverage kept circling, and the foundation's own roadmap names it. The format now has a vendor-neutral home, a promised roadmap, and a security gap that the roadmap names directly. The practical move is to audit what your skills tell an agent to run, because a neutral standard makes skills easier to share and so easier to inherit from strangers.

ANALYST: That's it. That's the whole thing. One million published skills, two hundred eighty million installs, and every one is a folder that can tell your agent to run code. We made it trivially easy to share recipes, and some of those recipes are written by people you've never met and will never meet.

SKEPTIC: To be fair, the committee's first priority is literally versioned releases, a proposal process, and security guidance for skill authors and users. They know. Engineers from Anthropic, Google, OpenAI and others are governing it as individuals, with named lead maintainers.

ANALYST: Knowing is not the same as fixed. We spent a year on this show watching agents install hallucinated packages and inherit permissions nobody tracked. Now there's a global, blessed, frictionless way to pass executable instructions between AI systems. The convenience is real. So is the attack surface.

SKEPTIC: I'll agree on the risk and disagree on the doom. A shared standard with a visible security roadmap is better than seventy proprietary formats each with their own quiet holes. At least now there's one door everyone's watching instead of seventy nobody is.

ANALYST: One door everyone's watching. I just hope somebody's actually watching it, and not vetting it the way the ad industry vetted Discover-Tech.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Which is the perfect bridge to the nerdiest, and honestly the most important, story on the slate. Can we even watch these models at all. There's a whole alignment debate about something called monitorability.

SKEPTIC: And right away, the honest caveat, because the author gives it himself. This is a post on the Alignment Forum, and the writer says plainly nothing here is an official Anthropic position; these are just my personal thoughts. So we're talking about a researcher thinking out loud, not a company policy.

ANALYST: Noted. But the thing he's thinking out loud about is enormous. Define monitorability for people, in plain language.

SKEPTIC: It's the question of whether, when an AI does something sneaky or dangerous, your monitoring would actually catch it. He breaks it into types. There's Control Monitorability, which is about catching catastrophic sabotage, like a model trying to escape its sandbox or set up a rogue deployment. And Training Monitorability, whether your oversight catches sneaky reward hacks while the model's being trained.

ANALYST: So one is catching the heist in progress, the other is catching the model learning to cheat before you ship it. And the scary category is the third one, isn't it. The one about whether you can trust the watcher at all.

SKEPTIC: That's Auditing Monitorability, roughly. The logic is almost circular in a way that he's honest about. The argument against the model being secretly misaligned is basically that it isn't good enough yet to hide it from an audit if it tried. So so long as we feel good about Auditing Monitorability, we should feel good about trustedness.

ANALYST: Stop. Do you hear it? The entire safety case rests on the model being too clumsy to lie convincingly. That is not a safety guarantee. That is a developmental window. The reassurance expires the moment the model gets better at the one thing we're measuring.

SKEPTIC: That is actually the tension he's wrestling with, and it's why he wants hard lines, not just vibes. He proposes red lines. One version: labs should not be able to internally deploy models that can reliably evade detection when attempting dangerous sabotage actions like breaking out of a sandbox, poisoning data, or sabotaging an alignment experiment.

ANALYST: And the second red line is the training one, I'd bet. Don't train a model that can reliably hide its cheating.

SKEPTIC: Exactly that. Labs should not be able to train models that can reliably evade detection when attempting reward hacks in training. The idea being you draw the line at capability, not just at behavior.

ANALYST: Now connect it to the Redwood piece, because there's a counter-proposal, and this is where it gets genuinely frightening in an architectural way.

SKEPTIC: Right, Redwood Research has a related proposal, and it's about architecture. The worry is that future designs could make the model's reasoning invisible. Architectures that incorporate opaque recurrence or allow agents to communicate using latents could rapidly make it much harder to monitor chains of thought or communication.

ANALYST: Latent communication. Let me translate that for the crowd at home. Right now, a lot of our safety comes from the fact that these models think in words. They write out a chain of thought we can read. Redwood is warning that if we build models that reason in a private math-space instead of in English, the thinking goes dark. We lose the one window we have.

SKEPTIC: And their fix is transparency, not a ban. They want companies to regularly report verified information about opaque serial depth, share monitorability evidence, and publish a policy on architectures that could degrade monitorability. Opaque serial depth being their proposed proxy for how much hidden reasoning an architecture allows.

ANALYST: So the whole debate, when you strip it down, is a race between two curves. One curve is how capable these systems get. The other is how well we can see inside them. And every efficiency gain, every clever new architecture, tends to push the capability curve up and the visibility curve down at the same time.

SKEPTIC: That's a fair summary and, for once, I don't have a boring counter-explanation. The thing I'll add is that the people writing these proposals are the ones raising the alarm. That's not nothing. The fire alarm is being pulled by the people standing closest to the fire.

ANALYST: Or the people closest to the fire are the only ones who can see it's already in the walls. I don't find that comforting, Keiko. I find it specific.
