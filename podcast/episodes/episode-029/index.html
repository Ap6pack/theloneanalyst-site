THE CALCULATOR THAT ISN'T YOURS

October 25, 2026 Episode 29

Duration: 25 minutes

[SEGMENT: cold_open]

ANALYST: Keiko, I need you to open the calculator on your laptop. Right now, humor me.

SKEPTIC: It's open. Two plus two is still four. Are we doing a bit before we've even said hello?

ANALYST: Did you open it, or did something open it for you?

SKEPTIC: I clicked the calculator. With my finger. Like a person who wanted to add some numbers.

ANALYST: Good. Because there's a piece of malware in the news this week where, when an investigator tries to open the infected files, the computer just quietly launches the calculator instead. Four functions. Blinking cursor. Nothing to see here.

SKEPTIC: Okay, that part is genuinely real, and I resent how much I enjoy it. It's almost polite.

ANALYST: It's the politeness that should terrify you. Welcome to the show.

[SEGMENT: intro]

ANALYST: You're listening to The Lone Analyst. I'm the Analyst, and no, that's still not a first name, that's a security posture.

SKEPTIC: And I'm Keiko Carrow, here to keep at least one of us tethered to the surface of the planet. Episode twenty-nine, we've got six stories, and they all sort of rhyme if you squint.

ANALYST: They rhyme if you don't squint, Keiko. That's the part keeping me up.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: So the calculator thing. That's a real bootkit. Kaspersky's research team found it, they're calling it BlackX, and it does something that is genuinely below the floor of your operating system.

SKEPTIC: Define below the floor, because that's the kind of phrase you use right before you lose me.

ANALYST: When you turn a computer on, before Windows even wakes up, there's a feature called Secure Boot that's supposed to check that nothing untrusted is loading. BlackX gets underneath that. It runs before the referee shows up to the game.

SKEPTIC: And it does that using an old flaw, not some brand-new zero-day. That's the part the reporting keeps emphasizing.

ANALYST: Right. It exploits a vulnerability called Baton Drop. CVE from 2022. This bug is four years old. It's the same hole an earlier bootkit called BlackLotus used back in 2022 and 2023.

SKEPTIC: And here's the detail I had to read twice. Kaspersky says the victims they found were on fully patched Windows machines. Updated. Current. Still got hit.

ANALYST: Because the fix isn't really a fix you get automatically. Microsoft's complete mitigation revokes the trust on the vulnerable signed bootloader, but that revocation doesn't just happen when you install updates. An administrator has to go flip a specific switch and run a task. If nobody flips it, the door stays open, officially patched or not.

SKEPTIC: So it's not that the lock is broken. It's that the lock comes with a second lock you have to install yourself, and almost nobody reads that far into the manual.

ANALYST: That's the whole pattern. And once BlackX is in, it loads a malicious driver that goes after security software specifically. The reporting names two vendors it interferes with, Kaspersky and Avast. It's reaching up through the floor to disable the smoke detectors.

SKEPTIC: And then the calculator trick.

ANALYST: And then the calculator trick. When anything tries to open the bootkit's own files, the driver intercepts that and returns a handle to calc dot exe instead. You go looking for the malware, and the system cheerfully shows you a pocket calculator. It's camouflage made out of the most boring app on your machine.

SKEPTIC: Who's getting hit with this? Because 'espionage campaign' can mean a lot of things.

ANALYST: The targets were diplomatic organizations in Central Asia. Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan. Early 2026. And Kaspersky attributes it, with medium confidence, to a group tracked as APT41, based on shared identifiers and some tradecraft patterns.

SKEPTIC: Medium confidence. I want to underline that, because you're about to sprint to a conclusion.

ANALYST: I'm not sprinting. I'm noting that the same four-year-old hole keeps getting reused because the fix depends on humans doing an extra manual step, and that is a dependency you can plan an entire espionage program around. That's not a leap, that's just reading the incentive.

SKEPTIC: That one I'll actually give you. The scary thing here isn't exotic. It's that 'patched' and 'protected' quietly stopped meaning the same thing, and the diplomats in three countries found out the hard way.

ANALYST: Patched is a checkbox. Protected is a verb. Somebody has to keep doing it.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Okay, keep that feeling, because the next one is worse in a quieter way. There was a poisoned release of a tool called LiteLLM. Microsoft's threat intelligence people did a full writeup on it.

SKEPTIC: LiteLLM. For people who aren't us, what is that, roughly?

ANALYST: It's a very popular piece of plumbing. It's a proxy and a toolkit that sits in front of a bunch of different AI model providers so developers can talk to all of them the same way. Which means the machines running it are usually holding the good stuff. Provider API keys, cloud credentials, database connection strings.

SKEPTIC: So it's the valet stand of an AI setup. It's holding everybody's keys by design.

ANALYST: Perfect way to put it. On October 21st, a compromised version, 1.107.0, got published to PyPI, which is the main Python package repository, and it also went out through official container images. And the way it steals credentials is the part I can't stop thinking about.

SKEPTIC: This is the thing where you don't have to actually use the package.

ANALYST: You don't have to import it. You don't have to run the app. The malicious version drops files that Python executes just during startup, through a hook. So in an affected environment, merely starting Python, running a pip command to install something totally unrelated, your code editor's language server quietly firing up, any of that can trigger the credential stealer.

SKEPTIC: And it collected a lot. The reporting lists SSH keys, cloud credentials, CI pipeline secrets, AI tool tokens, browser passwords, and the provider keys sitting in the LiteLLM database itself. Then it encrypts the haul and ships it out.

ANALYST: Ships it to a server dressed up to look like the project's own domain, which is a nice little psychological touch. And the backdoor piece could reach out and pull down more code to run later.

SKEPTIC: How did a compromised build even become the official build? Because that's the part that should scare the developers listening.

ANALYST: According to the maintainers, stolen developer GitHub credentials were used to alter the release workflow. And there's this lookalike repository detail. The real project lives at BerriAI, one word. The workflow got redirected toward Berri-AI, with a hyphen. One punctuation mark of difference.

SKEPTIC: A hyphen. The entire integrity of the supply chain came down to whether somebody noticed a hyphen.

ANALYST: And here's the response advice that tells you how bad it is. Microsoft explicitly says investigators should not start Python or pip inside a suspect environment just to check whether the bad package is there, because the act of checking can set off the payload.

SKEPTIC: You can't look in the box without the box going off. That's a genuinely nasty property for a thing to have.

ANALYST: And to their credit, PyPI pulled the release within a couple hours and the bad container images were taken down. The window wasn't open forever. But a couple hours in a system that auto-pulls dependencies thousands of times a minute is not nothing.

SKEPTIC: So the scale of exposure isn't about how long it lived, it's about how many machines reached for it reflexively while it was live.

ANALYST: Correct. And I want to connect it to the bootkit for one second without making it a conspiracy. Both stories are the same shape. A trusted thing, a signed bootloader, an official package, is the delivery vehicle. The attacker isn't breaking your trust. They're renting it.

SKEPTIC: That's a fair shape and I'll let it stand. I'd only add the boring institutional lesson, which is that the valet stand needed two people to sign off on the keys, and it turned out one stolen login could drive the car out.

ANALYST: One stolen login and a hyphen.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Let me steer us somewhere with a steering wheel, because I actually want to drive this one. EPIC published a piece this week about connected cars, and it's less about hackers and more about who your car tells on you to.

ANALYST: This is the privacy group, the Electronic Privacy Information Center. What's the hard number in it? You always have a hard number.

SKEPTIC: There's a study they cite from Northeastern University with Consumer Reports. They tested 21 vehicles and 30 companion phone apps. Nineteen of the 21 cars disclosed data about their drivers to third parties. And seven of the 30 apps handed sensitive personal information to companies tied to advertising and tracking.

ANALYST: Nineteen out of twenty-one. That's not a few bad apples, that's the orchard. The car is a rolling sensor package that reports driving behavior, location, in-car functions, and it does it by default.

SKEPTIC: And the part of the EPIC piece that stops being a tech story and becomes something heavier is the domestic violence angle. This is written around survivor safety.

ANALYST: Tell me, because this is where the usual privacy abstraction gets very concrete very fast.

SKEPTIC: The core problem is shared ownership. An abuser and a survivor may jointly own the vehicle. And connected-car features let someone remotely start the car, lock it, honk the horn, and pull precise location. So an abuser can track, harass, even trap someone, sometimes after a separation, sometimes after a protective order.

ANALYST: And the manufacturers hesitate to cut off access because, legally, it's a co-owned asset. So the privacy system defers to the paperwork, and the paperwork says the person doing the stalking is also an owner.

SKEPTIC: That's exactly the gap. The consent model was built for websites. You click 'I agree,' you're the one user. A car gets driven by a spouse who never opened the app, a teenager, a valet, eventually a used-car buyer. Nobody re-consents at any of those handoffs.

ANALYST: So the car accumulates permission like plaque. Every person who ever sat in it is folded into a consent that one account holder clicked years ago. And the data just keeps flowing to whoever holds that login.

SKEPTIC: EPIC's framing is that there's movement, though. Some state laws, some federal regulatory effort, some enforcement. But big gaps remain and the industry hasn't closed them.

ANALYST: Can I ask the uncomfortable design question? Why does the horn need to be internet-accessible at all. Why is 'honk from anywhere' a feature that shipped. Who was that for.

SKEPTIC: Honestly? It was built for the good version of the story. You lost your car in a parking garage, you tap the app, it honks, you find it. The feature isn't evil. It's just that the same remote honk works whether you're a frantic shopper or someone the court told to stay away.

ANALYST: That's the whole trap, though. Every one of these features has a sunny demo. Remote start so your car's warm. Location so your family feels safe. And each one is a dual-use tool where the second use is coercive control, and the car has no idea which driver it's serving.

SKEPTIC: And it can't know. That's the thing I don't think regulation fully grapples with yet. You can minimize the data, you can give survivors an off switch, but the vehicle itself has no concept of a restraining order.

ANALYST: It's a two-ton object that knows your location to the meter and understands your legal situation not at all. I'm not even doing a conspiracy here. I'm just describing the car.

SKEPTIC: No, that one you can say flat. There's no secret agency behind it. It's just a convenience feature that nobody stress-tested against the worst person who might use it.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Here's a tonal whiplash for you. Our next item is a company blog post, from Databricks, about fighting telecom fraud with what they're calling an AI coworker. And I want to read it two ways at once, which is the sales pitch and the actual numbers.

SKEPTIC: Let's separate those immediately, because it is a vendor post and vendors are selling something. What are the numbers that aren't theirs?

ANALYST: The headline figure comes from the Communications Fraud Control Association's 2025 survey. Telecom fraud losses have climbed to 41.82 billion dollars. And separately they cite that US consumers reported losing more than 12.5 billion to fraud in 2024, which was a 25 percent jump in a single year.

SKEPTIC: Okay, those are industry-group and government-style figures, not Databricks's own marketing math. I'll take those. The 41.82 billion lines up with the CFCA's loss survey, that checks out.

ANALYST: And the diagnosis in the post is actually the interesting bit, even through the sales gloss. They say the signal to catch most of this fraud already exists. It's just scattered across six or seven different systems that don't share one place to ask a question. Call records here, billing there, SIM and device data somewhere else, network logs somewhere else again.

SKEPTIC: So the fraudster isn't beating a wall. They're slipping through the seams between departments.

ANALYST: Exactly. And the timing mismatch is the kicker. They point out that the more sophisticated fraud often gets processed in overnight batches, while the actual attack window is measured in minutes. You're reviewing last night's tape while the robbery is happening now.

SKEPTIC: And then the pitch is, of course, buy our platform that unifies all of it. Lakebase, Genie, the agentic workflow, the AI coworker that triages cases. That's the part I read with my arms folded.

ANALYST: Fold your arms, sure. But I'll defend the underlying claim, which isn't really controversial. If your detection signal lives in seven databases and your decision has to be made in ninety seconds, then merging those databases is the single highest-leverage thing you can do. The AI coworker is almost a distraction from the real fix, which is plumbing.

SKEPTIC: That's the funniest part to me. The exciting 'AI coworker' branding is bolted onto what is, at its core, a story about getting your spreadsheets to talk to each other.

ANALYST: It's always plumbing, Keiko. Every glamorous AI story, you pull the panel off and it's a plumber reaching into a wall. And here's my one paranoid note, delivered responsibly. The same architecture that scores a fraudster in a millisecond is a real-time dossier on every ordinary customer. They're not doing anything improper with it, that's not the claim. The claim is that 'see every account instantly' is the capability, and fraud is the sympathetic first use.

SKEPTIC: And that's a fair observation as long as you keep it as an observation. The post is about fraud. It doesn't describe doing anything else with the data, and I'm not going to let you imply it does.

ANALYST: I'm not implying. I'm noting the shape, again. A capability shows up wearing its most defensible outfit.

SKEPTIC: Noted and bounded. The thing I'll actually credit them on is naming the real enemy, which is latency. Fraud moved to machine speed and a lot of defense is still running on the overnight bus schedule. That gap is real whether or not you buy their product.

ANALYST: The robbery's in minutes, the review's in the morning. Close that and you've done most of the work. Everything else is branding.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: This next one is my favorite of the slate and it's the least conspiratorial, so brace yourself. An MIT historian, Benjamin Lindquist, has a paper arguing that AI hallucinations aren't a modern bug. They were a design goal. On purpose. Decades ago.

ANALYST: Wait. On purpose. Somebody wanted the machine to make things up?

SKEPTIC: Not 'make things up' as in lie. 'Make unexpected leaps.' His argument, published in a history journal called Past and Present, is that mid-century engineers deliberately injected randomness into algorithms that were otherwise built to be perfectly deterministic. Because they thought a machine that could only produce predictable answers could never be creative.

ANALYST: So they associated intelligence with a little bit of chaos. With the illogical jump.

SKEPTIC: That's the thesis. He points to a Bell Labs engineer, John Pierce, in the 1950s, who was interested in how mathematics could introduce disorder into art. Break the cliché. Add a burst of the unexpected that just might lead to an insight. And early neural-network people loved these systems precisely because they'd give you strange, surprising outputs, not because they were always right.

ANALYST: This reframes the entire modern complaint. Everybody's out here filing bug reports against hallucination like it's a defect that crept in. And he's saying it's a seventy-year-old feature we're now trying to amputate.

SKEPTIC: That's basically his warning. He says companies now have real legal and commercial reasons to suppress the strangeness, because a confidently wrong answer is a lawsuit or a headline. But he argues sanding off the hallucination may also sand off the creative capacity. Same knob. You can't always turn down one without turning down the other.

ANALYST: There's a melancholy footnote in here too, isn't there. The neural-network approach lost for decades.

SKEPTIC: It did. Symbolic AI, the rules-and-propositions camp, dominated for years. And part of why the neural side faded, Lindquist notes, is the early death of one of its charismatic champions, the psychologist Frank Rosenblatt, who died at 43. The messy-mind approach lost its loudest voice right when it needed one.

ANALYST: So the entire character of the AI in your pocket, the fact that it dreams, the fact that it occasionally invents a court case that never happened, that traces back to a specific intellectual bet that creativity requires irrationality. And a guy at Bell Labs who wanted to rough up a painting with math.

SKEPTIC: And I want to be careful, because this is a historian's interpretation, not a law of physics. He's reading the lineage. But it's a well-sourced reading, and it does something useful. It turns 'why does the chatbot lie' into 'what did we originally want this thing to be.'

ANALYST: It also means the people demanding a perfectly reliable, perfectly truthful model are, in a sense, asking for the symbolic-AI dream in a neural-AI body. They want the rule-follower. They just want it to also be clever. And the whole history says pick one.

SKEPTIC: That's the trade-off he lands on. You can reduce hallucinations a lot. You probably can't get to zero without losing the surprising, inventive behavior that was the entire point of the architecture.

ANALYST: I have to say, this is the rare story where the deeper you dig, the less sinister it gets, and it's throwing off my whole rhythm.

SKEPTIC: Sit in the discomfort. Sometimes the machine making stuff up is just the machine doing the thing a man in 1955 specifically hoped it would do.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Last big one, and we're going to orbit. Two Georgetown analysts, Kathleen Curlee and Sam Bresnick, wrote a piece in Foreign Affairs about China's space program catching up to the US, commercially and militarily.

SKEPTIC: These are CSET researchers, the Center for Security and Emerging Technology. What's the specific event they're building on, because the op-ed is pegged to something concrete.

ANALYST: August 19th of this year. A Chinese aerospace company called LandSpace launched a rocket, and the booster, the big expensive bottom stage, detached and landed back on the ground intact. It was the first time a Chinese firm recovered a rocket on land. And that came about six weeks after the country's first-ever rocket recovery, which a big state-owned contractor pulled off at sea.

SKEPTIC: So two recoveries in a summer. The land one from a commercial startup, the sea one from the state giant. Why does landing the booster specifically matter so much? That's the reusability thing.

ANALYST: That's the whole ballgame. If you can land the booster, you can reuse it, and reuse is what collapses the cost of getting to orbit. Cheap launch is what lets you build megaconstellations, tens of thousands of satellites. China's stated it wants those. The missing piece was always a cheap ride up. This is them starting to solve the ride.

SKEPTIC: And the authors are careful not to overstate it. The US is still well ahead by the raw count.

ANALYST: Very careful. They note the United States still conducts more than twice as many launches and has more satellites in orbit than anyone. So this is not 'China passed us.' Their framing is 'the gap is closing and Washington's response has been muted.' Their line, and I'll quote it exactly, is that "The era of unassailable U.S. space power is ending, and Washington has no choice but to adapt."

SKEPTIC: Unassailable is the operative word there. Ending, not ended. They're describing a trend, not a finish line. I appreciate when the experts hedge in the actual sentence.

ANALYST: And the military half is what makes it a security story and not a cheerleading story. Space isn't just satellite internet. It's surveillance, communications, navigation, the coordination that modern militaries completely depend on. If China can both build out its own constellations and threaten yours, the whole backbone of how the US fights is suddenly contested.

SKEPTIC: Which is why their actual recommendation isn't just 'launch more.' It's a two-parter. Invest in space defense, and reduce reliance on the domain so you're not catastrophically exposed if it gets contested.

ANALYST: That second part is the mature one and nobody will quote it. Everyone wants 'build more rockets.' The harder advice is 'don't let your entire military run on something a rival can switch off.'

SKEPTIC: Now here's where I wait for you to find the shadowy pattern, because you've been suspiciously grounded for two stories.

ANALYST: I don't even need a shadow here. The through-line writes itself, and it's the same one from the bootkit. Dominance that was assumed is dependence that was never hardened. We assumed Secure Boot, we assumed space superiority, and in both cases the quiet assumption is the attack surface.

SKEPTIC: That's a genuine parallel and not a leap, so I'll allow it, reluctantly. The difference is that one is a registry flag an admin forgot and the other is two great powers and a reusable rocket. The scale is slightly different.

ANALYST: Scale's different. The human error is identical. Somebody assumed the lock would hold because it held yesterday.

SKEPTIC: On that we agree. Though I notice you got to say 'I told you so' without ever actually being told anything.
