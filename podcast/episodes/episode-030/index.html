ANALYST: A systemic insecurity. As opposed to the warm, personal, artisanal insecurity of handing one government a key to your messages. That's fine, apparently.

ANALYST: The next two hundred fifty years. I can't get my phone to survive a single operating system update, and we're planning the security posture for the year 2276.

SKEPTIC: The first one's almost boring, which is probably the point. Make security part of the architecture, secure by design, beginning with secure defaults, approved patterns, and clear trust boundaries, instead of bolting it on near the end.

ANALYST: Right, because the alternative is what we actually do, which is build the house, move in, and then install the locks by arguing with the furniture. Every breach I've ever read about is a security decision that lost a fight to a shipping deadline.

SKEPTIC: There's a second one you'll like, the inventory principle. Every organization needs a continuously updated inventory of its applications, open-source dependencies, development tools, AI models, and external services, with clear ownership for critical systems.

ANALYST: Know what you have. It sounds like a fortune cookie, but it's the one nobody can do. Half these companies couldn't tell you how many services are quietly running under their own name. Which, hey, callback to the last story, is exactly why a stranger can mail fraud through your pipeline. You can't defend an asset you forgot you owned.

SKEPTIC: And the one that's going to age into the headline is the code-trust principle. Verify every line of code, because code should earn trust whether it was written by an employee, generated by an AI assistant, or obtained from a third party.

ANALYST: Generated by an AI assistant. There it is. That's the quiet part. We've got a whole generation of code being written by something that's confident and wrong at the same time, and this list is gently suggesting maybe we read it before we ship it.

ANALYST: Forty-four percent. Flip a coin, and almost half the time the machine hands you working software with a hole pre-drilled in it. And the pitch for these tools is speed. We're shipping the holes faster. That's the productivity gain.

SKEPTIC: In fairness, a known vulnerability is one you can scan for. That's the whole argument for independent testing. The number's bad, but it's bad in a catchable way.

ANALYST: Sure, if anyone's catching. A principle that says verify every line only works if there's a human left in the building who knows what a line is supposed to do. My worry isn't the forty-four percent. It's the day nobody's checking because the machine seemed so sure.

SKEPTIC: That's a fair worry, and it's not even a conspiracy, which is upsetting for your brand. It's just a staffing question dressed up as a security one.

ANALYST: My brand contains multitudes. Some of them are just tired middle managers.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Speaking of machines grading machines, here's one that actually flips your usual complaint. There's a paper out of arXiv called PaperFormalizer. The pitch is that even a carefully proofread paper can leave small gaps in its proofs or assumptions, and this is a Lean-based agentic pipeline that helps authors find and close those gaps and turn the paper into a Lean-verified version.

ANALYST: Wait. Lean is the proof assistant. The thing where you can't move to the next line of math until the computer agrees the current line is actually true.

SKEPTIC: Right. It takes your LaTeX source, transcribes the paper into a dependency graph of statements, audits each statement for faithfulness, ambiguity and correctness, and then formalizes and proves them in Lean against Mathlib. Mathlib being the big library of already-verified math.

ANALYST: So it reads your paper, and instead of nodding along the way a reviewer does at two in the morning, it tries to actually rebuild every claim from verified parts, and the places it can't rebuild are the places you were quietly bluffing.

SKEPTIC: That's the idea, and here's the part I think you'll actually respect. It doesn't overrule the human. The pipeline stops and asks the author whenever a statement admits more than one reading, and presents every repair as a proposal the author can accept, decline, or redirect.

ANALYST: Huh. So the machine is the junior associate who flags the problem and says, boss, I can fix this two ways and they mean different things, which did you want. That's the opposite of everything we complained about tonight.

SKEPTIC: It really is. This whole episode's been software being confidently wrong, and this is a tool whose entire personality is refusing to guess what you meant.

ANALYST: What's it running on? Because if you tell me it's just another model...

SKEPTIC: It is, partly. PaperFormalizer runs inside a coding agent, either Claude Code or Codex, and it's available on GitHub. So the same kind of assistant that writes the 44-percent-buggy code is here being pointed at verifying math instead of producing it.

ANALYST: That's the fascinating tension, right? The same engine. In one story it's the forger. In this story it's the inspector. The difference is entirely what you chained it to. You put a proof checker at the end and suddenly the confident liar has to show receipts.

SKEPTIC: And it compounds in a nice way. The author's toolkit grows as more papers are formalized, and authors can share their verified papers so researchers who build on them can reuse the Lean code. So there's a registry, a public pile of papers that have actually been checked down to the logic.

ANALYST: Okay, I have to ask the paranoid version, because it's my job and I'd hate myself if I skipped it. Who decides what counts as verified? Because a seal of truth is a very powerful object, and whoever stamps it has a lot of quiet power.

SKEPTIC: That's a fair question, but the Lean part is the answer. The checker isn't vibes. It's a formal system. Either the proof type-checks against Mathlib or it doesn't. You can't charm it. That's kind of the whole appeal versus everything else we talked about.

ANALYST: No, that's the one thing tonight I don't want to drag. A machine that admits what it can't prove instead of bluffing. I didn't think I'd meet one this week. Whose names are on it, out of curiosity?

SKEPTIC: The authors are Jiyuan Tan, Vasilis Syrgkanis, and Jose Blanchet. And no, before you ask, there is no evidence any of them are a front for anyone. They're researchers.
