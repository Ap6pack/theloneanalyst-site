ANALYST: Start with the plumbing. PHP. Not glamorous, not an AI, it's the thing quietly running under an enormous number of content management systems. And two vulnerabilities dropped that let someone execute code on your server with no login at all.

SKEPTIC: Right. The security researchers at Unit 42 flagged the pair. Two critical vulnerabilities in PHP, both remote code execution, both rated 9.2 on the CVSS scale. Someone nicknamed the pair PHP2Shell.

ANALYST: Say that score out loud again for me.

SKEPTIC: Nine point two. Out of ten.

ANALYST: Out of ten, for the language under half the web's front doors. And the terrifying part isn't the score. It's how little you have to do to pull the trigger.

SKEPTIC: That's the part that actually got me. A working exploit showed that a single unauthenticated request can trigger the attack before any application code runs. You don't need an account. You don't need the app to cooperate.

ANALYST: And who's exposed? Basically everyone who serves a page.

SKEPTIC: More or less. Every PHP install that serves web requests is affected, including PHP-FPM, Apache with mod_php, php-cgi, FrankenPHP and LiteSpeed. The teams running old mod_php setups are in the worst spot.

ANALYST: Give me the scale. The number that made me close my eyes.

SKEPTIC: Palo Alto's own scanning. At the time of publication they identified over 22.6 million exposed instances potentially vulnerable to these two bugs.

ANALYST: Twenty-two million. That is not a vulnerability, that's a weather system. And here's where my paranoia earns its keep, because the researchers had a wobble. They put out a report, then pulled part of it back.

SKEPTIC: They did, and I'll give you the honest version, not your version. On October 25 Unit 42 retracted an October 23 report that attributed a European government agency intrusion to PHP2Shell, saying they couldn't confirm the initial access came from these flaws. That's a correction. That's what good disclosure looks like.

ANALYST: Or that's what it looks like when the first draft of history is too embarrassing to keep.

SKEPTIC: Or a careful team said the thing we can't prove yet and then unsaid it. Both can be true, and the scary reality didn't need the retraction. By a few days later, a campaign was reported chaining PHP2Shell with a Linux kernel privilege-escalation bug to get root, and Unit 42 said it independently verified the same attacker's tooling.

ANALYST: So the chain exists. Front door, then the whole house.

SKEPTIC: And the regulators moved. CISA added both of these to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on October 26. That's the government saying this is not theoretical, go patch.

ANALYST: And the fix. Tell them the uncomfortable part about the fix.

SKEPTIC: The uncomfortable part is there's no clever workaround that fully saves you. The releases fix two critical vulnerabilities that can allow unauthenticated remote code execution, and the project says there are no complete mitigations, updating is the only safe choice.

ANALYST: So the entire defensive strategy for a fifth of the web is please, everyone, at the same time, go upgrade a thing you forgot you were running.

SKEPTIC: Yes. And some won't, because they inherited a server from a contractor who left in 2019.

ANALYST: That's not a conspiracy. That's just sad. I almost prefer the conspiracies.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Stay right there on the word patch, because Microsoft put out its big annual threat report, and the headline finding is about who keeps getting hit.

SKEPTIC: The Digital Defense Report. Government agencies and services were the sector most impacted by cyber threats in 2026, accounting for 27% of observed activity, up from 17% in 2025.

ANALYST: Government, number one target, big jump. Fine. But Dark Reading ran a piece arguing the report has a convenient blind spot, and the blind spot is shaped exactly like the company that wrote it.

SKEPTIC: This is Andrew Grotto's argument, and I want to state it precisely. The report highlights government agencies as top targets for AI-driven attacks, but omits Microsoft's own role in that exposure.

ANALYST: The report talks a lot about AI. Agentic attackers, novel malware, phishing at scale. All real. But a government doesn't get breached by a vibe. It gets breached through a bug in software it already runs.

SKEPTIC: And the market concentration is the thing that makes that pointed. Microsoft holds about 85% market share in US public sector productivity software, and accounts for nearly 23% of known exploited vulnerabilities tracked by CISA since November 2021, far more than runner-up Cisco's 6%.

ANALYST: So one vendor is nearly a quarter of all the actually-exploited bugs on the government's radar, and the same vendor publishes the report about how the government keeps getting attacked, and somehow its own name doesn't come up.

SKEPTIC: I'll grant you that's a strange omission. I won't grant you that it's necessarily sinister. Companies don't tend to write their own vulnerabilities into their marketing reports. That's not a plot, that's a press office.

ANALYST: But it's a plot-shaped press office, Keiko. There's a detail in there about the VA that genuinely bothered me.

SKEPTIC: That one's grim regardless of framing. The VA dropped its Data Loss Prevention program after bundling security into a Microsoft license, putting 18 million veterans' records at risk.

ANALYST: So you cancel the thing watching the door because the new landlord says the door comes with a lock. And then eighteen million people are standing behind it.

SKEPTIC: And the track record isn't abstract. Since 2023, Chinese state-sponsored actors compromised Microsoft products in at least two significant instances, accessing over two dozen US government agencies' emails in one attack and at least seven agencies in another in July 2025.

ANALYST: So here's the part where I actually agree with the report and that scares me more. The report's own view is that AI isn't the whole monster.

SKEPTIC: Right. The 2026 report doesn't predict a sudden AI takeover of cybercrime; Microsoft's view is that AI makes familiar attacks faster and cheaper and adds agents as a new, often poorly governed attack surface. Faster, not magical.

ANALYST: Faster is the whole problem though. Boring bug plus fast AI equals the same break-in on a stopwatch.

SKEPTIC: That part the report actually owns. AI has let threat actors compress parts of the attack lifecycle from days to minutes. I just don't think naming the AI lets you skip naming the bug.

ANALYST: See, that's the agreement I didn't want. The flashy AI villain is a great way to not talk about the unpatched server.

SKEPTIC: We can both be suspicious of the framing and still tell people the fix is unglamorous. Patch your stuff. It's the least cinematic advice on earth.

[SEGMENT: story]

SKEPTIC: Different flavor of story. Google DeepMind and the UK government are expanding an AI tool for planning departments. This is the boring bureaucracy of whether you can build a loft conversion, and they think AI can speed it up.

ANALYST: What's it called and what does it actually touch?

SKEPTIC: It's called PlanAssist. It was developed by Google DeepMind with the UK government, Google Cloud and the AI consultancy Faculty, aimed at routine residential planning reviews. It targets the document checks and policy reviews behind loft conversions, extensions and conservatories, drafting cited assessments that qualified officers can edit while they keep responsibility for every decision.

ANALYST: Give me the numbers they're selling it on.

SKEPTIC: Across the pilot councils, the average time an officer spent on householder assessment tasks fell by 58%. And routine householder applications make up as much as 70% of planning officers' work, even though they're often simple to approve.

ANALYST: Okay. And then there's the number that made the back of my neck do a thing.

SKEPTIC: I know which one. More than 70% of the reports the tool generated were approved by officers with no edits.

ANALYST: Seventy percent. No edits. So the human in the loop reads the robot's homework and goes yep, looks right, seven times out of ten, without touching a word. That's not oversight. That's a signature line.

SKEPTIC: Or seven times out of ten the robot got a genuinely routine case genuinely right, and the officer's judgment kicked in on the hard three. You're assuming rubber stamp. The design assumes triage.

ANALYST: The design always assumes triage. Then the backlog shows up, and triage quietly becomes the whole job.

SKEPTIC: That's a fair worry and I'll say it plainly, because even the coverage flagged it. DeepMind reports the 58% and the 70%, but neither figure establishes faster end-to-end decisions or independently verified accuracy. These are the company's pilot numbers, not an outside audit.

ANALYST: Thank you. That's the part that matters. The vendor grades its own exam and then scales it.

SKEPTIC: And it is scaling. On October 31 DeepMind said PlanAssist is moving into a 12-month beta with 12 more English councils, 15 in total, after the Barnet, Camden and Dorset pilots. Subject to successful testing, the plan is to make it available to every English planning authority in 2027.

ANALYST: And this isn't just a Britain thing, is it. There's a franchise model.

SKEPTIC: There is. DeepMind has framed this explicitly as a model for other governments, part of what it calls National Partnerships for AI, with similar deals signed with Singapore, South Korea and India.

ANALYST: So one company's model gets to sit inside the decision about whether your neighbor can build the extension that blocks your light, in four countries, with the officer as the last human whose job is increasingly to agree.

SKEPTIC: And I'll still defend the part worth defending. A qualified planning officer will continue to review and approve each assessment before a decision is issued. The statutory call stays with a person.

ANALYST: On paper.

SKEPTIC: On paper is where accountability lives, Analyst. That's the only place you can actually hold someone to it.

ANALYST: I'll give you that one. I don't love it, but I'll give it to you.

[SEGMENT: story]

ANALYST: Okay, this next one I have been waiting to say out loud, because it's a paper that quietly takes a flamethrower to a pile of celebrated science. The claim is that a lot of fancy deep learning results in big journals weren't actually beating anything.

SKEPTIC: Slow down. Give me what it actually did. The authors assembled 36 supervised classification and regression tasks from peer-reviewed studies in Nature, Science, and Cell-family journals that investigate novel deep learning methods for scientific applications.

ANALYST: Thirty-six real, published, peer-reviewed, celebrated results. And then they did the mean thing. They asked an AI to build a dumb version.

SKEPTIC: A simple baseline, yes. They investigated whether a large language model could automate the construction of baselines. The point isn't that the fancy model is fake. The point is that sometimes nobody checked it against a boring alternative.

ANALYST: And the boring alternative shows up, cracks its knuckles, and the result is brutal. Hit me with it.

SKEPTIC: The automatically constructed baselines recovered a median of 95.5%, mean 85.7%, of the gap between a trivial predictor and the score of the deep learning model. And the kicker, the median simple baseline matched or exceeded the deep learning model in 12 of the studies.

ANALYST: Twelve out of thirty-six. A third of these breakthroughs, matched or beaten, by a model an LLM wrote as a sanity check. That's not an AI story, Keiko, that's an emperor's-new-clothes story with footnotes.

SKEPTIC: It's a methodology story, and that's actually the more important one. This is an old, boring, true problem. Establishing the value of a novel deep learning method requires appropriate comparison. People skipped the comparison, so progress looked bigger than it was.

ANALYST: But think about what that does to the whole pipeline. We trust these results. Companies cite them. Funding follows them. And a third of them might just be a complicated way of saying water is wet.

SKEPTIC: That's the leap I won't fully take. Recovering most of the gap isn't the same as the method being worthless. A fancy model that's barely better than simple can still be better, and the paper's own framing is careful about that. It's a check, not a verdict.

ANALYST: A check that an AI performs on the AI hype. There's something almost beautiful about using the machine to catch the machine's own press releases.

SKEPTIC: That part I'll agree with, genuinely. The authors suggest automated baseline construction can provide a useful check on the predictive contributions of supervised deep learning methods. Cheap skepticism, at scale, pointed at your own field. That's good hygiene.

ANALYST: Cheap skepticism at scale is my entire personality. I feel seen by a preprint.

SKEPTIC: Don't get attached. It'll match-or-beat you by a median of 95.5% too.

ANALYST: Ten million a year. See, that's the stat that reframes everything. We spend all night being paranoid about machines reading our email, and here's the same technology aimed at the thing that's actually quietly coming for everybody.

SKEPTIC: And it shifted what AI is even doing in the lab. In a 2025 study in Cell, the researchers moved from filtering known compounds to using generative AI to design new antibiotic structures. From searching to inventing.

SKEPTIC: Last story, and I want us to be careful with this one, because it's not a bit. An Egyptian court shut down one of the country's last independent newsrooms and convicted its editor.

ANALYST: Managing an unlicensed website. Sit with that phrase. That's the charge. Not a lie they printed. Not a fact they got wrong. A license.

SKEPTIC: There is. This conviction was for the unlicensed-site charge. There's a separate track. The original reporting notes she faces a second trial, on a charge of spreading false news, which has become one of the government's go-to tools against journalists.

ANALYST: That's the pattern I can't make a joke out of, and I'm not going to. You arrest the fact-checkers, then you arrest the people who stood up for the fact-checkers, and you call all of it a licensing matter.

SKEPTIC: And the response wasn't quiet.
